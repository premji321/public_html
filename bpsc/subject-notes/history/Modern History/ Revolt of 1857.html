THE REVOLT OF 1857

Complete Notes for BPSC & UPSC Prelims

Subject: Modern Indian History | Difficulty: Advanced

Focus: Important facts, causes, leaders, centres, chronology, consequences and PYQ concepts

1. Introduction

The Revolt of 1857 was a major uprising against British rule in India. It began as a revolt of Indian soldiers (sepoys) in the Bengal Army but developed into a broader rebellion involving rulers, peasants, zamindars and sections of the civilian population in several regions.

It challenged the authority of the East India Company and ultimately led to the transfer of Indian administration to the British Crown in 1858.

2. Background of the Revolt

By the middle of the nineteenth century, the East India Company had expanded its territorial control across large parts of India. British expansion created resentment among different sections of Indian society.

  • Annexation of Indian kingdoms.
  • Displacement of rulers and traditional elites.
  • Exploitative land-revenue arrangements.
  • Economic difficulties among peasants and artisans.
  • Discontent among Indian soldiers.
  • Fear of interference in religious and social customs.
  • Racial discrimination and exclusion from higher positions.

3. Causes of the Revolt of 1857

A. Political Causes

Doctrine of Lapse

Associated especially with Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856), the policy enabled the British to annex certain states when a ruler died without a recognised natural male heir, where an adopted heir was not accepted for succession.

  • Satara — 1848
  • Sambalpur — 1849
  • Jhansi — 1854
  • Nagpur — 1854
Important distinction: Awadh was annexed in 1856 on the grounds of alleged misgovernment, not under the Doctrine of Lapse.

Annexation of Awadh, 1856

The British accused Nawab Wajid Ali Shah's administration of misgovernment. The annexation displaced the Nawab, angered sections of the aristocracy and taluqdars, and caused resentment among soldiers with connections to Awadh.

Nana Sahib's grievance

Nana Sahib, adopted son of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II, was denied continuation of his father's pension. He later became a prominent leader at Kanpur.

B. Economic Causes

  • Peasants: Heavy land-revenue demands, indebtedness and risk of losing land created hardship.
  • Artisans: Machine-made British goods competed with Indian products, while royal patronage declined.
  • Zamindars and taluqdars: Annexations and revenue changes deprived some landholders of privileges.
  • Traditional elites: Declining royal courts reduced employment and patronage for soldiers, artists, craftspeople and administrators.

C. Military Causes

  • Indian soldiers often received lower pay and fewer privileges than British soldiers.
  • Indians faced restrictions on promotion and senior military command.
  • The General Service Enlistment Act, 1856 required new recruits to accept the possibility of overseas service.
  • Changes in allowances and service conditions created resentment.
  • Religious and cultural rumours increased mistrust.

D. Religious and Social Causes

Missionary activity, some social and educational changes, and rumours of forced conversion created anxiety among sections of society. The revolt was not simply a religious uprising; political, economic and military grievances were also important.

E. Immediate Cause: Greased-Cartridge Controversy

The Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle cartridge had to be bitten open before loading. Rumours spread that cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat, offending religious sensitivities among many Hindu and Muslim soldiers.

Begum Hazrat Mahal — Lucknow: Led resistance in Awadh; her son Birjis Qadr was proclaimed ruler.

Rani Lakshmibai — Jhansi: Died fighting near Gwalior in June 1858.

Kunwar Singh — Bihar: Leader associated with Jagdishpur; regained it in April 1858 and died on 26 April 1858.

Tantia Tope: Continued resistance through mobile military operations; executed in 1859.

Important regional details

Delhi: Rebels reached Delhi on 11 May 1857 and declared Bahadur Shah II their symbolic leader. The British recaptured Delhi in September 1857. Bahadur Shah II was exiled to Rangoon, where he died in 1862.

Kanpur: Nana Sahib emerged as principal rebel leader. Tantia Tope played a major military role and continued resistance after the British recovered Kanpur.

Jhansi: Rani Lakshmibai fought British forces and later joined the struggle in central India. She died fighting near Gwalior in June 1858.

Bihar — Kunwar Singh: He was approximately 80 years old in 1857, regained Jagdishpur in April 1858 and died on 26 April 1858. His brother Amar Singh continued resistance.

BPSC special focus: Kunwar Singh is one of the most important personalities for Bihar-specific questions on the Revolt of 1857.

6. Why Did the Revolt Fail?

  1. Lack of unified leadership: No single central authority coordinated all rebel forces.
  2. Limited geographical spread: The revolt was strongest in northern and central India.
  3. No common programme: Different groups had different objectives.
  4. British military advantages: Organisation, communications, logistics and reinforcements favoured the British.
  5. Support from some Indian rulers: Several states supported the British or remained neutral.
  6. Limited resources: Rebels struggled to obtain weapons, ammunition, funds and supplies.
  7. Lack of coordination: Rebel operations were not coordinated effectively across regions.
Exam conclusion: The revolt failed because of fragmented leadership, uneven participation, limited coordination and British military advantages—not because the rebels lacked courage or genuine grievances.

7. Nature of the Revolt: Historical Interpretations

InterpretationExplanation
Sepoy MutinyEmphasises the role of Indian soldiers in initiating the revolt.
Popular rebellionHighlights participation of civilians, peasants, taluqdars and displaced elites in several regions.
First War of IndependenceEmphasises the anti-colonial character and later nationalist significance.
Regional and varied uprisingRecognises that participation, objectives and intensity differed across India.

Important historians and writers

  • V. D. Savarkar: Popularised the First War of Indian Independence interpretation in his 1909 work.
  • S. N. Sen: Examined the causes and development of the revolt in Eighteen Fifty-Seven.
  • R. C. Majumdar: Emphasised the varied nature of the uprising and challenged the idea that it was uniformly a national war of independence.
Exam caution: No single interpretation is accepted by every historian. The revolt's character must be assessed regionally and historically.

8. Consequences of the Revolt of 1857

A. End of East India Company rule

The Government of India Act, 1858 transferred administration from the East India Company to the British Crown.

B. Queen Victoria's Proclamation, 1858

The proclamation announced the new framework of Crown rule and gave assurances concerning religious beliefs, treaties with Indian princes and eligibility for public employment. These assurances did not end colonial discrimination.

C. Secretary of State for India

The Act created the office of Secretary of State for India, assisted by a Council of India, strengthening direct control from Britain.

D. Governor-General and Viceroy

Lord Canning became the first Viceroy under Crown rule. The Governor-General continued to administer British India, while “Viceroy” emphasised his role as the Crown's representative.

E. Changes in the army

  • The British increased the proportion of British soldiers in key formations.
  • They maintained tighter control over artillery.
  • Recruitment favoured selected regions and communities.
  • Army organisation aimed to prevent another coordinated uprising.

F. Policy towards princely states

The British became more cautious about annexation and relied more on alliances with Indian princes. Crown policy also addressed recognition of adoption rights.

G. Changes in British attitudes

The revolt increased racial suspicion and hostility. Colonial administration increasingly emphasised security and preservation of British authority.

10. BPSC Prelims: High-Probability Questions

Q1. The major outbreak of the Revolt of 1857 began at: A) Delhi B) Meerut C) Kanpur D) Lucknow
Answer: B) Meerut.
Q2. Who was the Mughal emperor during the revolt? A) Akbar II B) Shah Alam II C) Bahadur Shah II D) Farrukhsiyar
Answer: C) Bahadur Shah II.
Q3. Awadh was annexed in 1856 on grounds of: A) Doctrine of Lapse B) Alleged misgovernment C) Subsidiary Alliance D) Customs duties
Answer: B) Alleged misgovernment.
Q4. Who led the revolt in Bihar? A) Nana Sahib B) Kunwar Singh C) Khan Bahadur Khan D) Ahmadullah Shah
Answer: B) Kunwar Singh.
Q5. Who was Governor-General when the revolt began? A) Dalhousie B) Canning C) Ripon D) Curzon
Answer: B) Lord Canning.
Q6. The Government of India Act, 1858 transferred administration to: A) Mughal emperor B) British Crown C) Congress D) Princely states
Answer: B) The British Crown.
Q7. Who led resistance at Lucknow? A) Rani Lakshmibai B) Begum Hazrat Mahal C) Annie Besant D) Sarojini Naidu
Answer: B) Begum Hazrat Mahal.
Q8. The Doctrine of Lapse is most closely associated with: A) Ripon B) Dalhousie C) Curzon D) Wavell
Answer: B) Lord Dalhousie.
Q9. The immediate trigger was: A) Rowlatt Act B) Vernacular Press Act C) Greased-cartridge controversy D) Partition of Bengal
Answer: C) Greased-cartridge controversy.
Q10. Who became the first Viceroy under Crown rule? A) Dalhousie B) Canning C) Mountbatten D) Ripon
Answer: B) Lord Canning.
