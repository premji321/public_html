Chapter Overview

The expansion and consolidation of British power in India was a gradual historical process through which a European trading company transformed itself into a major territorial and political power.

The English East India Company initially came to India for commercial purposes. However, political instability, military superiority, diplomatic strategies, economic resources and internal rivalries among Indian rulers enabled the Company to expand its influence.

Central Theme: The transformation of the English East India Company from a trading organisation into a territorial power and the establishment of British political supremacy in India.

Major Stages of British Expansion

  1. Establishment of trading centres and factories.
  2. Political intervention in Bengal.
  3. Military victories at Plassey and Buxar.
  4. Expansion into Mysore and Maratha territories.
  5. Annexation of Sindh and Punjab.
  6. Expansion through Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse.
  7. Extension of British influence towards neighbouring regions.

Meaning of Expansion and Consolidation

  • Expansion: Extension of territorial boundaries and political influence.
  • Consolidation: Strengthening and stabilising political control over conquered territories.
  • Colonialism: Political and economic domination of one country by another.
  • Imperialism: Policy of extending a country's power and influence over other territories.

Nature of British Expansion

British expansion in India was not achieved in a single battle. It involved a combination of military, political, economic and diplomatic methods.

  • Military conquest and annexation.
  • Interference in the succession disputes of Indian states.
  • Formation of alliances with Indian rulers.
  • Economic exploitation and control over revenue.
  • Use of treaties and diplomatic pressure.
British supremacy emerged through a combination of military victories, political manipulation, economic power and administrative control.

English East India Company

  • The English East India Company was established in 1600.
  • It received a royal charter from Queen Elizabeth I.
  • The charter granted the Company trading privileges in the East.
  • Its initial objective was to participate in the profitable Asian trade.

1. Political Disunity

  • There was no single powerful political authority controlling the entire Indian subcontinent.
  • Several regional kingdoms competed with each other.
  • Internal conflicts weakened the resistance of Indian states.

2. Military Organisation

  • The Company maintained a disciplined army.
  • European military techniques and training provided advantages.
  • Effective use of artillery and infantry strengthened British forces.

3. Economic Resources

  • British commercial interests provided financial resources.
  • Revenue collected from conquered territories financed further expansion.
  • Control over Bengal's wealth became especially important.

4. Diplomatic Strategies

  • The Company exploited rivalries among Indian rulers.
  • It supported particular claimants in succession disputes.
  • It used treaties and alliances to increase its influence.

5. Weaknesses of Indian Political Powers

  • Rivalries among regional rulers.
  • Lack of coordination against the Company.
  • Frequent succession disputes.
  • Financial and administrative weaknesses in some states.

Importance of Bengal

Bengal was one of the richest provinces of the Mughal Empire. Its agricultural production, textile industry and commercial activities made it economically significant.

Economic Importance

  • Fertile agricultural land.
  • Large-scale production of cotton and silk textiles.
  • Important inland and overseas trade.
  • Major commercial centres such as Murshidabad, Dhaka and Calcutta.
  • Revenue from agriculture and trade.

Alivardi Khan

  • Alivardi Khan became the Nawab of Bengal in 1740.
  • He faced repeated Maratha invasions.
  • He attempted to maintain the independence of Bengal's administration.
  • He died in 1756.

Siraj-ud-Daulah

  • Siraj-ud-Daulah succeeded Alivardi Khan in 1756.
  • He was the last independent Nawab of Bengal before the establishment of Company dominance.
  • He opposed the misuse of trading privileges by the English East India Company.

Reasons for Conflict with the Company

  • Unauthorised fortification of Calcutta.
  • Misuse of trade privileges and dastaks.
  • Interference in the internal affairs of Bengal.
  • Giving protection to people who had conflicts with the Nawab.
The conflict between Siraj-ud-Daulah and the Company ultimately led to the Battle of Plassey in 1757.

Background

The dispute between the Nawab of Bengal and the English East India Company intensified during the rule of Siraj-ud-Daulah.

Causes of the Battle

  1. Misuse of trade privileges by Company officials.
  2. Fortification of Fort William without the Nawab's permission.
  3. Company interference in Bengal's internal politics.
  4. Conspiracy against Siraj-ud-Daulah.

Course of the Battle

  • The battle was fought on 23 June 1757.
  • The opposing forces were led by Siraj-ud-Daulah and Robert Clive.
  • Mir Jafar and other conspirators did not effectively support the Nawab.
  • The Nawab's army was weakened by betrayal and lack of coordination.
  • Siraj-ud-Daulah was defeated.

Installation as Nawab

  • Mir Jafar was installed as Nawab after the Battle of Plassey.
  • His position depended heavily on the support of the East India Company.
  • He had to fulfil the Company's financial and political demands.

Company's Demands

  • Financial compensation.
  • Commercial privileges.
  • Territorial concessions.
  • Political influence over Bengal.

Removal and Replacement

  • Mir Jafar could not satisfy the growing demands of the Company.
  • The Company replaced him with Mir Qasim in 1760.
  • Mir Jafar was later restored as Nawab.
Mir Jafar's installation demonstrated how the Company could influence the succession and political authority of Bengal.

Rise of Mir Qasim

  • Mir Qasim became Nawab of Bengal in 1760.
  • He was expected to cooperate with the Company.
  • He attempted to strengthen his administration and financial independence.

Administrative Measures

  • He shifted his capital from Murshidabad to Munger.
  • He reorganised the army.
  • He attempted to improve revenue collection.
  • He sought to reduce the Company's interference.

Conflict over Trade Privileges

  • Company servants misused dastaks for private trade.
  • Indian merchants faced unfair competition.
  • Mir Qasim abolished internal duties to establish equality between Indian and Company traders.
  • The Company opposed this measure because it weakened its commercial advantages.

Outcome

The conflict between Mir Qasim and the Company escalated into armed confrontation. Mir Qasim joined forces with the Nawab of Awadh and the Mughal Emperor.

Background

Mir Qasim formed an alliance with Shuja-ud-Daulah, the Nawab of Awadh, and Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II against the East India Company.

Background

After the Battle of Buxar, the Company negotiated settlements with Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II and Shuja-ud-Daulah.

Important Provisions

  • Shah Alam II granted the Diwani rights of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa to the East India Company.
  • The Company obtained the right to collect revenue from these provinces.
  • Shuja-ud-Daulah was restored to his position as Nawab of Awadh subject to agreed conditions.
  • Awadh became an important buffer state between the Company's territories and the north-western regions.

Meaning of Diwani

  • Diwani referred to the right to collect revenue and manage civil financial administration.
  • It gave the Company access to a major source of income.
  • The Company's commercial organisation now possessed major territorial revenue powers.
Exam Fact: In 1765, Robert Clive obtained the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa from Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II.

Introduction

Robert Clive introduced the system of Dual Government in Bengal in 1765.

Meaning

Under this system, the Company exercised real authority while the Nawab formally retained responsibility for certain administrative functions.

Division of Responsibilities

DepartmentAuthority
DiwaniEast India Company — revenue collection and civil financial authority.
NizamatNawab — formally associated with criminal justice, police and executive administration.

Features

  • The Company controlled the revenue.
  • The Nawab retained nominal administrative responsibility.
  • The Company exercised substantial political authority without accepting full administrative responsibility.
  • Indian officials continued to perform many administrative functions under Company supervision.

Consequences

  • Administrative confusion.
  • Corruption and exploitation.
  • Revenue pressure on peasants.
  • Weakening of accountability.
  • Contribution to the Bengal famine of 1770 through multiple interacting causes, including administrative failures and severe economic distress.

End of Dual Government

  • Warren Hastings ended the Dual Government in 1772.
  • The Company assumed more direct responsibility for administration.

Introduction

The Marathas emerged as one of the most powerful political forces in India after the decline of Mughal authority.

Important Centres of Power

  • Peshwa: Pune.
  • Scindia: Gwalior.
  • Holkar: Indore.
  • Gaekwad: Baroda.
  • Bhonsle: Nagpur.

Political Structure

  • The Peshwa was the leading minister and later became the central political figure.
  • Major Maratha chiefs exercised substantial regional authority.
  • Internal rivalries weakened collective political action.

Conflict with the British

The British gradually exploited divisions within the Maratha Confederacy and fought three major wars to establish supremacy.

Background

  • The conflict arose out of a succession dispute involving the Peshwa's position.
  • The British supported Raghunath Rao.
  • Maratha leaders opposed British interference.

Course and Outcome

  • The war began in 1775.
  • The Company faced strong Maratha resistance.
  • The conflict ended with the Treaty of Salbai in 1782.

Treaty of Salbai (1782)

  • It restored a temporary balance between the British and Marathas.
  • Madhavrao II was recognised as Peshwa.
  • Raghunath Rao's position was settled through the treaty arrangements.
  • The treaty secured a period of relative peace between the two powers.

Background

  • Internal conflicts weakened the Maratha Confederacy.
  • Peshwa Baji Rao II sought British assistance.
  • He signed the Treaty of Bassein in 1802.

Treaty of Bassein (1802)

  • Signed between Peshwa Baji Rao II and the British.
  • The Peshwa accepted a subsidiary alliance arrangement.
  • British troops were stationed to protect the Peshwa.
  • The treaty greatly increased British influence over Maratha politics.

Course of the War

  • Scindia and Bhonsle opposed the treaty and British interference.
  • The British fought major campaigns in western and central India.
  • Holkar also came into conflict with the British.
  • Several treaties were concluded with Maratha chiefs.

Results

  • British influence over the Maratha Confederacy increased.
  • Scindia and Bhonsle lost important territories.
  • The Peshwa became increasingly dependent on the British.

Background

  • The Peshwa and several Maratha chiefs resented British interference.
  • The Company sought to establish complete political supremacy.
  • The conflict developed into a final struggle between the Company and the Marathas.

Course

  • The war began in 1817.
  • The British defeated the Peshwa's forces and other Maratha powers.
  • British military victories ended the remaining political independence of the Peshwa.

Results

  • The Peshwa's office was abolished in 1818.
  • Baji Rao II was pensioned and sent to Bithur.
  • Most Peshwa territories came under Company control.
  • The Maratha Confederacy ceased to function as a major united political power.

Main Reasons

  1. Internal Rivalries: Maratha chiefs frequently competed with one another.
  2. Lack of Unity: They failed to maintain a united front against the British.
  3. British Diplomacy: The Company exploited internal political divisions.
  4. Military Organisation: British forces had advantages in discipline and organisation.
  5. Treaty of Bassein: It weakened the independence of the Peshwa.
  6. Economic Resources: The Company could finance prolonged military campaigns.
The defeat of the Marathas in 1818 was a major step towards the establishment of British supremacy in India.

Meaning

Paramountcy referred to the claim of the British Crown or Company to supreme authority over Indian princely states.

Main Features

  • The British claimed superior political authority.
  • Indian rulers were expected to accept British supremacy.
  • The Company increasingly interfered in the internal affairs of princely states.
  • The policy provided ideological and political justification for expansion.

Historical Significance

  • It weakened the sovereignty of Indian rulers.
  • It strengthened British control over princely states.
  • It was used to justify intervention and annexation.

Meaning

The Ring-Fence Policy aimed to protect Company territories by maintaining buffer states around them and supporting neighbouring rulers against external threats.

Objectives

  • Protection of British territories.
  • Creation of buffer zones.
  • Limiting the influence of rival powers.
  • Increasing British influence in neighbouring regions.

Historical Importance

The policy represented an early stage of British strategic expansion beyond direct territorial annexation.

Introduced by

Lord Wellesley (Governor-General, 1798–1805) greatly expanded the use of the Subsidiary Alliance system.

Meaning

Under the system, an Indian ruler accepted the stationing of British troops in his territory and agreed to bear the cost of maintaining them.

Main Conditions

  1. The ruler had to maintain a British force or pay for it.
  2. The ruler could not employ other European powers without British approval.
  3. The ruler could not enter into certain diplomatic relations without British consent.
  4. A British Resident was stationed at the ruler's court.
  5. The ruler often had to surrender territory or revenue to meet the cost of the subsidiary force.

Effects

  • Indian rulers lost much of their military independence.
  • British influence increased over internal and external affairs.
  • States became financially dependent on the Company.
  • British troops could be stationed across Indian territories.

Important States Associated with the System

  • Hyderabad (1798).
  • Mysore (1799, after the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War).
  • Awadh (1801 arrangement).
  • Peshwa Baji Rao II — Treaty of Bassein (1802).
Exam Fact: Lord Wellesley is closely associated with the expansion of the Subsidiary Alliance system.

Introduced by

Lord Dalhousie (Governor-General, 1848–1856) used the Doctrine of Lapse to annex several princely states.

Meaning

Under this policy, the Company refused to recognise an adopted heir as the legitimate successor to a ruler in certain cases, and annexed the state if the ruler died without a recognised natural male heir.

The Great Game

The term Great Game is used for the strategic rivalry between the British and Russian Empires in Central Asia during the nineteenth century.

British Objectives

  • Maintain a friendly government in Kabul.

The First Anglo-Afghan War is remembered for the disastrous British retreat from Kabul in 1842.

Meaning

Masterly Inactivity was a cautious foreign policy that avoided unnecessary intervention in Afghanistan and preferred maintaining a defensive position.

Associated Governor-General

Lord Lawrence (1864–1869) is particularly associated with this policy.

Main Features

  • Avoid direct interference in Afghan internal politics.
  • Maintain friendly relations with Afghanistan.
  • Avoid costly military adventures.
  • Protect British India through a cautious frontier policy.

Meaning

The policy of Proud Reserve involved maintaining a dignified distance from Afghanistan while avoiding excessive interference in its internal affairs.

Associated Governor-General

Lord Northbrook (1872–1876) followed a cautious approach towards Afghanistan.

Main Features

  • Respect for Afghan independence in internal matters.
  • Limited diplomatic involvement.
  • Avoidance of unnecessary political pressure.
  • Maintaining a stable frontier.

Background

  • Russian diplomatic influence in Afghanistan increased British anxiety.
  • The Afghan ruler Sher Ali Khan resisted British pressure.
  • The British launched an invasion in 1878.

Course

  • The British invaded Afghanistan in 1878.
  • Sher Ali Khan died in 1879.
  • The Treaty of Gandamak was signed in 1879.
  • British influence over Afghan foreign relations increased.

  • Abdur Rahman Khan emerged as Amir of Afghanistan.
  • The British withdrew their forces after the conflict.
  • Afghanistan retained internal independence while its foreign relations remained subject to British influence.

Importance

  • The north-western frontier was considered strategically important for the defence of British India.
  • It was vulnerable to political and military developments in Central Asia.
  • British policy aimed to maintain stability along the frontier.

Main Objectives

  • Prevent rival imperial powers from threatening India.
  • Maintain friendly relations with frontier rulers.
  • Control important passes and strategic routes.
  • Protect British territories.

Diwani

Right to collect revenue and manage civil financial administration.

Nizamat

Executive and criminal administration, including functions associated with law and order.

Dastak

A pass or permit that allowed duty-free trade under the Company's privileges. Its misuse by Company servants became a major source of conflict in Bengal.

Subsidiary Alliance

A political arrangement under which an Indian ruler accepted British troops and British influence over important diplomatic matters.

Doctrine of Lapse

A policy used by Lord Dalhousie to annex certain states where the Company refused to recognise adopted heirs as legitimate successors.

Paramountcy

The claim of superior political authority over Indian princely states.

Ring-Fence Policy

A defensive strategy involving buffer states and protection of Company territories.

Great Game

Strategic rivalry between the British and Russian Empires in Central Asia.

Buffer State

A territory situated between two competing powers, helping to reduce direct confrontation.

Dual Government

The arrangement under which the Company controlled revenue while the Nawab retained nominal administrative responsibilities in Bengal.

1. Commercial Beginning

The English East India Company came to India for trade and gradually established factories and trading settlements.

2. Political Entry through Bengal

The Company's interference in Bengal's politics led to the Battle of Plassey in 1757 and the Battle of Buxar in 1764.

3. Revenue and Administrative Control

The Treaty of Allahabad in 1765 gave the Company Diwani rights over Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. The Dual Government system followed.

4. Expansion in Southern India

The Company fought four Anglo-Mysore Wars against Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan. The final war ended with Tipu Sultan's death in 1799.

5. Expansion in Western and Central India

Three Anglo-Maratha Wars weakened the Maratha Confederacy and ended the Peshwa's political authority in 1818.

6. Expansion in North-Western India

The British annexed Sindh in 1843 and Punjab in 1849.

7. Political Annexation Policies

The Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse enabled the Company to expand its influence and territories.

8. Annexation of Awadh

Awadh was annexed in 1856 on the ground of alleged misgovernment, increasing resentment before the Revolt of 1857.

9. Expansion Beyond India

British strategic and political interests extended towards Nepal, Bhutan, Burma, Tibet and Afghanistan.

Final Conclusion

The expansion of British power in India was a gradual process. Through military victories, diplomatic alliances, political intervention, economic control and annexation policies, the English East India Company transformed itself from a trading organisation into a dominant territorial power.
