Chapter Overview

The expansion and consolidation of British power in India was a gradual historical process. The East India Company initially came to India for trade, but gradually became a major political and territorial power.

  • Commercial expansion and political intervention.
  • Battle of Plassey (1757) and Battle of Buxar (1764).
  • Conquest of Mysore, Maratha territories, Sindh and Punjab.
  • Administrative policies: Ring-Fence, Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse.
  • British relations with Nepal, Bhutan, Burma, Tibet and Afghanistan.
Central Theme: British expansion was achieved through military power, diplomacy, financial strength, political interference and exploitation of divisions among Indian rulers.

1. The British Imperial History

−

1.1 Meaning of Imperialism

  • Imperialism refers to the policy through which a powerful country extends its political, economic and military control over other territories.
  • Britain gradually established an extensive empire across different parts of the world.
  • India became one of the most important territories of the British Empire.

1.2 Importance of India for Britain

  • Rich agricultural resources.
  • Valuable raw materials.
  • Large domestic market for British manufactured goods.
  • Profitable trade opportunities.
  • Strategic geographical location in Asia.

1.3 Two Phases of British Imperial History

First Empire

  • Mainly extended across the Atlantic Ocean.
  • Focused on America and the West Indies.

Second Empire

  • Began around 1783, following the Peace of Paris.
  • British imperial expansion increasingly shifted towards Asia and Africa.

1.4 Ideological Foundation

  • British imperial ideology was influenced by the belief that European societies were more civilised than Asian and other non-European societies.
  • British intellectuals frequently justified imperial expansion through the supposed responsibility of civilising other populations.
  • Imperialism also served commercial, military and strategic interests.

2. Was the British Conquest of India Accidental or Intentional?

−

2.1 Accidental Theory – John Seeley

  • Historian John Seeley argued that the British conquest of India was largely accidental.
  • The British initially came to India for commercial purposes.
  • They did not originally intend to establish a vast territorial empire.
  • Political instability among Indian rulers gradually involved the Company in local conflicts.
  • Territorial expansion occurred while protecting commercial interests.
Important expression: The conquest of India took place in a “fit of absent-mindedness”.

2.2 Intentional Theory

  • Another group of historians argued that the British had a deliberate intention of establishing an empire in India.
  • The Company gradually interfered in Indian politics.
  • It exploited conflicts among Indian rulers.
  • It used military force and diplomacy to acquire territories.

2.3 Balanced Interpretation

  • Initially, the Company mainly sought to protect and promote commercial interests.
  • Political rivalries among Indian rulers provided opportunities for intervention.
  • Gradually, Company officials realised that they could expand their influence by supporting one ruler against another.
  • Later, British politicians and administrators consciously pursued imperial expansion.

2.4 Factors Behind British Expansion

  • Commercial profits from Indian trade.
  • Personal ambitions of Company officials.
  • Desire for territorial power.
  • Political instability in India.
  • Rivalry with European powers, particularly France.
  • Strategic considerations involving France and Russia.
Exam Point: British expansion initially developed through commercial and political circumstances, but later became increasingly imperialistic.

Historical Explanation

  • 1740 marks the beginning of intensified European rivalry.
  • 1757 marks the beginning of British political supremacy in Bengal.
  • 1761 marks a major transformation in the balance of Indian political power.
  • The political transformation took approximately eighty years to complete.
Conclusion: The British conquest of India was a gradual process rather than the result of a single battle.

4. Causes of British Success in India

−

The British took approximately a century to establish and consolidate their political authority over India. Their success resulted from military, administrative, economic and diplomatic advantages.

4.1 Superior Arms, Military Technology and Strategy

  • British firearms were generally superior to those used by many Indian rulers.
  • Their weapons included muskets and cannons.
  • British weapons offered advantages in firing speed, range and military effectiveness.
  • Several Indian rulers imported European weapons and employed European officers.
  • However, Indian military organisations often remained dependent on European models.

4.2 Better Military Discipline and Regular Salaries

  • The Company maintained a regular salary system for its soldiers.
  • Officers and troops were subjected to strict military discipline.
  • Regular payments helped maintain loyalty.
  • Many Indian rulers could not pay their soldiers regularly.
  • Soldiers sometimes became dissatisfied and mercenaries frequently changed sides.
  • The Marathas sometimes conducted campaigns specifically to collect revenue for paying troops.

4.3 Civil Discipline and Fair Selection System

  • British recruitment and appointments were generally based on skill and reliability.
  • Officers were selected according to merit.
  • Strict discipline was maintained.
  • In several Indian administrations, appointments were influenced by caste, clan and personal relationships.
  • Personal ambitions affected loyalty and administration.

4.4 Brilliant Leadership and Second-Line Leaders

Important British leaders:

  • Robert Clive.
  • Warren Hastings.
  • Elphinstone.
  • Munro.
  • Lord Dalhousie.

Important British military leaders:

  • Sir Eyre Coote.
  • Lord Lake.
  • Arthur Wellesley.

Capable Indian leaders included Haidar Ali, Tipu Sultan, Chin Qulich Khan, Madhu Rao Sindhia and Yashwant Rao Holkar. However, political rivalries and lack of sustained unity weakened resistance.

4.5 Strong Financial Backup

  • The Company earned substantial profits from overseas trade.
  • Its income helped finance military campaigns in India.
  • Britain possessed considerable financial and material resources.
  • British naval superiority helped transport soldiers, weapons and military supplies.

4.6 Nationalist Pride and Political Disunity

  • British people possessed a strong sense of national pride.
  • Indian political powers remained divided among different kingdoms and regional authorities.
  • There was no unified political organisation capable of consistently opposing British expansion.
Six Major Causes: Superior arms, military discipline, merit-based selection, strong leadership, financial and naval superiority, and political disunity among Indian powers.

5. British Conquest of Bengal

−

5.1 Bengal on the Eve of British Conquest

  • Bengal was the richest province of the Mughal Empire.
  • It included territories corresponding to present-day Bangladesh, West Bengal, Bihar and Odisha.
  • Bengal possessed a prosperous agricultural, manufacturing and commercial economy.

5.2 Important Exports from Bengal

  • Saltpetre.
  • Rice.
  • Indigo.
  • Pepper.
  • Sugar.
  • Silk.
  • Cotton textiles.
  • Handicrafts.

5.3 Commercial Importance

  • Approximately 60% of British imports from Asia consisted of goods from Bengal, according to the chapter.
  • The Company established factories at Balasore, Hooghly, Kasimbazar, Patna and Dacca.
  • By the 1690s, the English commercial settlement at Calcutta had been established.
  • The Company paid ₹3,000 annually to the Mughal emperor for trading privileges.

  • Agriculture and trade expanded.
  • Major cities became prosperous.

6. Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757)

−

6.1 Alivardi Khan and the English

  • Alivardi Khan was initially the Deputy Governor of Bihar.
  • In 1741, he defeated and killed Sarfaraz Khan.
  • He established himself as Nawab of Bengal after securing recognition from Mughal emperor Muhammad Shah.
  • He ruled until 1756.
  • He fought against Maratha incursions into Bengal.
  • Alivardi Khan died in April 1756 and his grandson Siraj-ud-daula succeeded him.

6.2 Challenges Before Siraj-ud-daula

  • Shaukat Jang – Nawab of Purnea and political rival.
  • Ghasiti Begum – hostile aunt of Siraj.
  • Mir Jafar – commander of the army.
  • Jagat Seth – influential banker.
  • Omichand – influential court figure.
  • Rai Ballabh and Rai Durlabh – important opponents.

6.3 Administrative Measures Taken by Siraj

  • Defeated and killed Shaukat Jang.
  • Confiscated the treasures of Ghasiti Begum.
  • Removed Mir Jafar from his position.
  • Appointed Mir Madan as commander.
  • Appointed Mohan Lal as an important administrator.

6.4 Causes of Conflict with the English

  • Misuse of trade privileges by the Company.
  • Fortification of Calcutta without the Nawab's permission.
  • Company interference in Bengal's internal politics.
  • Shelter provided to Krishna Das, who had fled with valuable treasures.
  • Competition between the English and French in Bengal.

6.5 Black Hole Tragedy

  • Siraj-ud-daula captured the English fort at Calcutta.
  • A story later became widely known as the Black Hole Tragedy.
  • According to the traditional account, 146 English persons were imprisoned in a small room and 123 died from suffocation.
  • Historians have questioned the story and suggested that the actual number of victims may have been much smaller.

6.6 Course of the Battle

  • Robert Clive arrived at Calcutta with a strong force from Madras.
  • He established a secret alliance with important opponents of Siraj.
  • The conspirators included Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, Jagat Seth and Omichand.
  • Mir Jafar was promised the Nawabship of Bengal.
  • The Company expected substantial rewards in return.

Historical Significance: Plassey laid the territorial foundation of British rule in India and marked the beginning of British political dominance in Bengal.

7. Mir Kasim and Treaty of 1760

−

7.1 Why Was Mir Jafar Replaced?

  • Mir Jafar became increasingly dissatisfied with British interference.
  • He entered into a conspiracy with the Dutch at Chinsura.
  • The Dutch were defeated by the English at Bedara in November 1759.
  • Mir Jafar failed to meet the financial demands of the Company.
  • The Company began supporting Mir Kasim, the son-in-law of Mir Jafar.

7.2 Treaty of 1760

  • Mir Kasim agreed to transfer Burdwan, Midnapur and Chittagong to the Company.
  • The Company received half the share in the chunam trade of Sylhet.
  • Mir Kasim agreed to pay outstanding dues.
  • He promised ₹5 lakh towards the Company's military expenditure in southern India.
  • The friends and enemies of Mir Kasim would be treated as friends and enemies of the Company.
  • Restrictions were placed on tenants moving between the Nawab's and Company's territories.

7.3 Administrative Reforms of Mir Kasim

  • Shifted the capital from Murshidabad to Munger in Bihar.
  • Wanted to maintain greater distance from Company interference at Calcutta.
  • Reorganised the bureaucracy.
  • Reorganised the army to improve military efficiency.
  • Attempted to strengthen the authority of the Nawab.

8. Battle of Buxar (22 October 1764)

−

8.1 Causes of Conflict

  • The main dispute involved the misuse of the Company's duty-free trade privileges.
  • The English Company possessed the right to trade in Bengal without paying transit dues or tolls.
  • Company servants extended these privileges to their private trade.
  • Some Company servants sold dastaks to Indian merchants for commissions.
  • These practices reduced the Nawab's revenue.

8.2 Meaning of Dastak

  • Dastak was a trade permit that exempted specified goods from payment of duties.
  • The Company used its privileges for private commercial activities.
  • Indian merchants faced unequal competition.
  • The Bengal government suffered financial losses.

8.3 Immediate Cause

  • Mir Kasim abolished internal duties to create equal trading conditions.
  • The English opposed this measure because they wanted preferential treatment.
  • Conflict developed between the Nawab and the Company in 1763.
  • The English won several battles at Katwah, Murshidabad, Giria, Sooty and Munger.

8.4 Formation of the Confederacy

After his defeats, Mir Kasim fled to Awadh and formed an alliance with:

  • Shuja-ud-daulah – Nawab of Awadh.
  • Shah Alam II – Mughal Emperor.

  • The English defeated the combined forces of three important Indian political authorities.

9. Treaty of Allahabad (August 1765)

−

9.1 Treaty with Shuja-ud-daulah

  • The Nawab of Awadh agreed to surrender Allahabad and Kara to Shah Alam II.
  • He agreed to pay ₹50 lakh to the Company as war indemnity.
  • Balwant Singh, Zamindar of Banaras, was granted full possession of his estate.

9.2 Treaty with Shah Alam II

  • The Mughal emperor agreed to reside at Allahabad under Company protection.
  • He issued a farman granting the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa to the Company.
  • He was to receive an annual payment of ₹26 lakh.
  • The Company received ₹53 lakh in return for Nizamat functions in these provinces, as described in the chapter.

9.3 Important Terms

  • Diwani: Right to collect revenue and administer revenue-related affairs.
  • Nizamat: Police, military defence and administration of justice.
  • Farman: Official royal order or decree.
  • Buffer State: A state situated between two larger political powers, helping separate their territories.

9.4 Significance

  • The Company received the Diwani rights of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
  • It obtained a formal legal basis for revenue collection.
  • Awadh became a buffer state between British territories and external threats.
  • The Mughal emperor became politically dependent on the Company.
  • The Company's financial position improved considerably.

10. Dual Government in Bengal (1765–1772)

−

10.1 Introduction

  • After the Battle of Buxar, the Company became the real political authority in Bengal.
  • Robert Clive introduced the Dual Government system.
  • It involved the nominal participation of the Nawab and the actual control of the Company.

10.2 Meaning of Dual Government

  • The Company controlled the revenue administration.
  • The Company also controlled the Nizamat through its authority to nominate the deputy subahdar.
  • The Nawab retained the appearance of governmental authority.
  • The Company exercised actual sovereign power.

10.3 Administrative Arrangements

  • Diwani rights were acquired from the Mughal emperor.
  • Nizamat authority was exercised through the Nawab's administration.
  • Mohammad Reza Khan was appointed Deputy Diwan of Bengal.
  • Raja Sitab Roy was appointed Deputy Diwan of Bihar.
  • Mohammad Reza Khan also acted as Deputy Nazim or Deputy Subahdar.

10.4 Defects of the System

  • The Company controlled revenue but avoided direct responsibility for administration.
  • The Nawab lacked adequate financial and military authority.
  • Neither authority effectively protected public welfare.
  • Administrative responsibility became unclear.
  • The system contributed to administrative breakdown.

10.5 End of Dual Government

  • Warren Hastings abolished the Dual Government system in 1772.
Exam Fact: Dual Government in Bengal operated from 1765 to 1772.

11. Mysore's Resistance to the Company

−

11.1 Background: Wodeyar Dynasty

  • After the Battle of Talikota in 1565, the Vijayanagara Empire declined.
  • Several smaller kingdoms emerged from its remnants.
  • In 1612, a Hindu kingdom under the Wodeyar dynasty emerged in Mysore.
  • Chikka Krishnaraja Wodeyar II ruled from 1734 to 1766.
  • Mysore later emerged as a powerful state under Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan.

11.2 Why Did the British Consider Mysore a Threat?

  • Mysore was a strong military power in southern India.
  • It maintained relations with the French.
  • It controlled important trade routes along the Malabar coast.
  • Its expansion threatened British interests around Madras.
  • Its military strength challenged the Company's influence in southern India.

12. Rise of Haidar Ali

−

12.1 Early Life

  • Haidar Ali was born in 1721.
  • He belonged to an ordinary family.
  • He began his career as a horseman in the Mysore army.
  • He served under Nanjaraj and Devaraj.
  • Despite having little formal education, he possessed strong military and administrative abilities.

12.2 Rise to Power

  • Mysore faced repeated incursions by the Marathas and the Nizam.
  • These invasions weakened its financial position.
  • Haidar Ali used military skill and diplomacy to strengthen his position.
  • He became the de facto ruler of Mysore in 1761.

12.3 Military Reforms

  • Established an arms factory at Dindigul with French assistance.
  • Introduced Western methods of military training.
  • Strengthened artillery and improved cavalry.
  • Captured Dod Ballapur, Sera, Bednur and Hoskote between 1761 and 1763.
  • Brought several Poligars of southern India under submission.

12.4 Conflict with the Marathas

  • Madhavrao-led Maratha forces attacked Mysore.
  • Haidar Ali suffered defeats in 1764, 1766 and 1771.
  • He paid large sums to secure peace.
  • After Madhavrao's death in 1772, Haidar recovered territories through campaigns during 1774–1776.

13. Four Anglo-Mysore Wars

−

13.1 First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769)

Causes

  • The English wanted to increase their influence in southern India.
  • The Company concluded an agreement with the Nizam of Hyderabad in 1766.
  • The Nizam transferred the Northern Circars to the English.
  • The English promised protection against Haidar Ali.
  • Haidar had territorial disputes with the Nawab of Arcot and differences with the Marathas.

Changing Alliances and Course

  • Initially, the Nizam, Marathas and English formed an alliance against Haidar Ali.
  • Haidar used diplomacy to weaken the alliance.
  • He paid the Marathas to remain neutral.
  • He promised the Nizam a share in conquered territories.
  • The Nizam subsequently became Haidar's ally.
  • Haidar Ali suddenly appeared before the gates of Madras.
  • The English faced pressure and were forced to negotiate peace.

Treaty of Madras (4 April 1769)

  • Exchange of prisoners.
  • Mutual restoration of conquered territories.
  • English promise to assist Haidar Ali if attacked by another power.
Result: The war ended without a decisive British victory.

13.2 Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780–1784)

Causes

  • The English failed to help Haidar Ali when the Marathas attacked him in 1771.
  • Haidar considered this a violation of the Treaty of Madras.
  • France supplied military materials to Mysore through Mahe.
  • The British attempted to capture Mahe.
  • Haidar regarded Mahe as being under his protection.

Course of the War

  • Haidar Ali formed an anti-English alliance with the Marathas and the Nizam.
  • He attacked the Carnatic and captured Arcot.
  • He defeated the English army under Colonel Baillie in 1781.
  • Sir Eyre Coote succeeded in separating the Marathas and Nizam from Haidar's side.
  • Haidar suffered defeat at Porto Novo in November 1781.
  • He later regrouped his forces and defeated the English, capturing their commander Braithwaite.

Death of Haidar Ali

  • Haidar Ali died on 7 December 1782. His son Tipu Sultan continued the war.

Treaty of Mangalore (March 1784)

  • Both sides agreed to restore the territories captured from each other.
  • The war ended without a decisive outcome.

13.3 Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–1792)

Causes

  • Travancore purchased Jalkottal and Cannanore from the Dutch.
  • These territories were connected with the Cochin state, a feudatory of Tipu.
  • Tipu considered the purchase a violation of his sovereign rights.
  • In April 1790, Tipu declared war against Travancore.
  • The English supported Travancore and attacked Tipu.

Course of the War

  • Tipu defeated the English under General Meadows in 1790.
  • In 1791, Lord Cornwallis took command.
  • The British captured Bangalore in March 1791.
  • They marched towards Seringapatam.
  • Coimbatore was captured but later lost.
  • The British attacked Seringapatam with support from the Marathas and the Nizam.

Treaty of Seringapatam (1792)

  • Nearly half of Mysore's territory was divided among the victors.
  • The English received Baramahal, Dindigul and Malabar.
  • The Marathas received territories around the Tungabhadra and its tributaries.
  • The Nizam received territories between the Krishna and beyond the Pennar.
  • Tipu had to pay ₹3 crore as war indemnity.
  • Two sons of Tipu were taken as hostages until payment was completed.

13.4 Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799)

Causes

  • Tipu wanted to recover territories lost under the Treaty of Seringapatam.
  • He opposed British expansion.
  • He maintained relations with the French.
  • Lord Wellesley wanted to eliminate Mysore's independent political power.
  • Wellesley wanted Tipu to accept the Subsidiary Alliance.

Course of the War

  • The war began on 17 April 1799.
  • British forces were led by General Stuart and General Harris.
  • Arthur Wellesley also participated.
  • The English received support from the Marathas and the Nizam.
  • The final battle took place at Seringapatam.
  • Tipu Sultan died fighting on 4 May 1799.

Consequences

  • Mysore's independent military resistance ended.
  • The British confiscated Tipu's treasures.
  • Tipu's family members were interned at Vellore.
  • The British restored the old Hindu Wodeyar dynasty.
  • Krishnaraja III became the ruler under British protection.
  • The new Mysore ruler accepted the Subsidiary Alliance.

13.5 Mysore After Tipu

  • Some territories were distributed among the British, Marathas and Nizam.
  • The Nizam received Gooty and Gurramkonda.
  • The British acquired Kanara, Wynad, Coimbatore, Dwaraporam and Seringapatam.
  • In 1831, William Bentinck took control of Mysore on grounds of misgovernment.
  • In 1881, Lord Ripon restored the kingdom to its ruler.

13.6 Tipu Sultan: Important Contributions

  • Popularly known as the Tiger of Mysore.
  • Born in November 1750.
  • Son of Haidar Ali.
  • Promoted military modernisation.
  • Organised his army on the European model.
  • Used French officers for military training.
  • Developed rocket technology and wrote a military manual explaining the operation of rockets.
  • Promoted sericulture in Mysore.
  • Established a Board of Admiralty in 1796.
  • Planned a fleet of 22 battleships and 20 large frigates.
  • Established three dockyards: Mangalore, Wajedabad and Molidabad.
  • Supported the establishment of a Jacobin Club at Seringapatam in 1797.
  • Adopted the title Citizen Tipu.
  • Planted the Tree of Liberty at Seringapatam.
  • Provided funds for the repair of the Sringeri Temple and installation of the idol of Goddess Sarada.

14. Anglo-Maratha Struggle for Supremacy

−

14.1 Rise of the Marathas

  • The decline of the Mughal Empire created opportunities for the Marathas.
  • They controlled extensive territories and collected tribute from several regions.
  • By the middle of the eighteenth century, they had become one of the most powerful political forces in India.
  • They exercised considerable influence in the Mughal court.

14.2 Third Battle of Panipat (1761)

  • The Marathas were defeated by Ahmad Shah Abdali.
  • The defeat weakened Maratha influence in northern India.
  • The Marathas later recovered much of their strength.
  • The death of Peshwa Madhavrao I in 1772 further weakened the authority of the Peshwas.

14.3 Maratha Confederacy

Bajirao I developed a confederacy of powerful Maratha chiefs.

15. First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782)

−

15.1 Background

  • After the death of Madhavrao in 1772, Narayanrao became Peshwa.
  • His uncle Raghunathrao assassinated him and attempted to become Peshwa.
  • Narayanrao's widow gave birth to a son named Sawai Madhavrao.
  • Twelve Maratha chiefs, known as the Barabhai Council, supported the infant Peshwa.
  • Nana Phadnavis became an important leader of the regency.

15.2 Treaty of Surat (1775)

  • Raghunathrao sought British support.
  • He transferred Salsette and Bassein to the English.
  • He transferred part of the revenues of Surat and Bharuch.
  • The English promised 2,500 soldiers in return.

15.3 Treaty of Purandhar (1776)

  • The British Calcutta Council opposed the Treaty of Surat.
  • Colonel Upton was sent to negotiate.
  • The Treaty of Purandhar was signed with the Maratha regency.
  • Raghunathrao was renounced as Peshwa and promised a pension.

15.4 Treaty of Wadgaon (1779)

  • The Maratha army under Mahadji Sindhia trapped the English near Talegaon.
  • The Marathas attacked British supply lines.
  • They adopted a scorched-earth strategy.
  • The English were surrounded at Wadgaon.
  • They surrendered in January 1779.
  • The Bombay government agreed to relinquish territories acquired since 1775.

15.5 Treaty of Salbai (1782)

  • Warren Hastings rejected the Treaty of Wadgaon.
  • British forces continued military operations.
  • Mahadji Sindhia proposed a new settlement.
  • The Treaty of Salbai was signed in May 1782.

Major Provisions

  • Salsette remained under British control.
  • Territories acquired after the Treaty of Purandhar were restored to the Marathas.
  • The English stopped supporting Raghunathrao.
  • The Peshwa agreed to grant Raghunathrao a maintenance allowance.
  • The English received trade privileges.
  • The Peshwa agreed not to support another European power.
  • Mahadji Sindhia acted as guarantor of the treaty.
Result: Peace was established between the two sides for approximately twenty years.

  • He gave up claims to chauth over the Nizam's territories.
  • The Company would arbitrate disputes with the Nizam and Gaekwad.
  • The Peshwa could not employ Europeans from countries at war with Britain.
  • His foreign relations came under British control.

16.3 Course of the War

  • Sindhia and Bhonsle opposed British expansion.
  • Arthur Wellesley defeated their combined armies.

18. Why Did the Marathas Lose?

−

18.1 Inept Leadership

  • Weak leadership affected political and military decision-making.
  • Personal rivalries weakened collective resistance.

18.2 Defective Nature of the State

  • The political system lacked effective institutional stability.
  • The authority of the central leadership was limited.

18.3 Loose Political Structure

  • The Maratha confederacy consisted of powerful regional chiefs.
  • These chiefs frequently pursued independent interests.
  • Their unity was temporary.

18.4 Inferior Military System

  • British military organisation was more disciplined.
  • British forces possessed advantages in artillery and military coordination.

18.5 Unstable Economic Policy

  • Financial weaknesses affected military expenditure.
  • Irregular revenue collection created difficulties.

18.6 Superior British Diplomacy and Espionage

  • The British exploited rivalries among Indian rulers.
  • They entered into separate treaties with individual chiefs.
  • They used diplomatic negotiations to isolate opponents.

18.7 Progressive British Outlook

  • The British possessed stronger institutional and organisational arrangements.
  • Their military and administrative systems were more coordinated.
Seven Reasons: Inept leadership, defective state structure, loose political set-up, inferior military system, unstable economic policy, superior British diplomacy and progressive British outlook.

19.3 Significance

  • British territorial influence expanded towards the north-west.
  • The conquest strengthened the British strategic position along the Indus.
Exam Fact: Conquest of Sindh – 1843; Governor-General – Lord Ellenborough; British commander – Charles Napier.

23. Extension of British Paramountcy

−

23.1 Meaning of Paramountcy

  • Paramountcy refers to the supreme political authority exercised by one power over other states.
  • The British expanded their paramountcy through military conquest, diplomatic agreements, administrative intervention, annexation and political treaties.

23.2 Two Methods of British Expansion

Method 1: Annexation through War

  • Bengal.
  • Mysore.
  • Maratha territories.
  • Sindh.
  • Punjab.

Method 2: Annexation through Diplomacy and Administration

  • Ring-Fence Policy.
  • Subsidiary Alliance.
  • Doctrine of Lapse.

24. Policy of Ring-Fence

−

24.1 Introduced by

  • Warren Hastings.

24.2 Meaning

  • The policy aimed to protect British territories by creating buffer zones around them.
  • The Company helped defend neighbouring states against external threats.
  • These states had to bear the financial cost of maintaining the defending forces.

24.3 Objectives

  • Protect Bengal from external invasions.
  • Prevent threats from Afghan invaders.
  • Counter the growing influence of the Marathas.
  • Strengthen the security of Company territories.

24.4 Example: Awadh

  • The Company agreed to defend Awadh's frontiers.
  • The Nawab had to pay the expenses of the defending army.
  • The security of Awadh was considered important for the security of Bengal.

24.5 Significance

  • The British expanded their influence without directly annexing every neighbouring state.
  • Indian rulers became financially dependent on the Company.
  • The policy developed into a more comprehensive system under Lord Wellesley.

25. Subsidiary Alliance

−

25.1 Introduced and Developed by

  • Lord Wellesley, Governor-General from 1798 to 1805.

25.2 Meaning

Under the Subsidiary Alliance system, an Indian ruler accepted British military protection in exchange for political and financial concessions.

25.3 Major Provisions

  • The ruler had to maintain a permanent British military force in his territory.
  • The ruler had to pay a subsidy for its maintenance.
  • A British Resident was stationed at the ruler's court.
  • The ruler could not employ other European nationals without British approval.
  • The ruler could not conduct wars or enter into treaties with other Indian rulers without British consultation.
  • The British promised protection against external enemies.
  • The British formally promised non-interference in the internal affairs of the allied state.

25.4 Objectives of Wellesley

  • Expand British political influence.
  • Prevent the revival of French influence.
  • Counter the possibility of French military intervention.
  • Establish British military bases at strategic locations.
  • Make Indian rulers dependent on the Company.

25.5 Four Stages of Evolution

  1. The Company offered military assistance to friendly Indian states.
  2. British and Indian forces jointly participated in military campaigns.
  3. The Indian ruler was required to provide money instead of soldiers. The Company maintained a fixed military force under British officers.
  4. The financial subsidy was fixed at a high level. If the ruler failed to pay, territory was transferred to the Company.

26. Doctrine of Lapse

−

26.1 Introduced and Applied Extensively by

  • Lord Dalhousie, Governor-General from 1848 to 1856.

26.2 Meaning

  • Under the Doctrine of Lapse, if an Indian ruler died without a natural male heir, the British could refuse to recognise an adopted son as the successor to the throne.
  • The British claimed the right to decide whether the state should pass to an adopted heir or be annexed.
  • An adopted son could inherit private property, but the British claimed that he did not automatically inherit the state.

26.3 Important Features

  • The policy was used to annex Indian princely states.
  • It undermined traditional succession practices of several Indian ruling families.
  • It increased British territorial control.
  • It created widespread dissatisfaction among Indian rulers.

26.4 Was Dalhousie the Originator?

  • Dalhousie was not the original creator of the doctrine.
  • Similar principles had been applied earlier.
  • However, Dalhousie applied the policy aggressively during his tenure.

27. Annexation of Awadh (1856)

−

27.1 Background

  • Awadh was one of the oldest states under the Subsidiary Alliance.
  • The system created financial pressure on the Nawab.
  • The state suffered from administrative problems.
  • Heavy taxes and illegal exactions affected the population.
  • British military expenses placed additional pressure on the treasury.

27.2 British Investigation

  • Lord Dalhousie directed Resident Sleeman to inspect the state.
  • Sleeman submitted a report describing administrative disorder.
  • Outram succeeded him as Resident in 1854.
  • Outram supported the earlier assessment.

27.3 Annexation

  • The British Court of Directors ordered annexation.
  • Nawab Wajid Ali Shah refused to sign away his rights.
  • The British abolished the throne in 1856.
  • Wajid Ali Shah was exiled to Calcutta.

27.4 Significance

  • Awadh was annexed on the grounds of misgovernment.
  • The annexation created resentment among the royal family, nobility, soldiers and population.
  • It contributed to the difficulties faced by the British during the Revolt of 1857.

29. Anglo-Bhutanese Relations

−

29.1 Background

  • British occupation of Assam in 1826 brought them into closer contact with Bhutan.
  • Disputes arose over border raids and frontier territories.
  • The British became involved in conflict with Bhutan.

29.2 Events

  • Bhutanese raids affected adjoining territories in Assam and Bengal.

30. Anglo-Nepalese Relations

−

30.1 Background

  • The Gorkhas established their control over Nepal around 1760.
  • They expanded southwards beyond the Himalayan region.
  • British annexation of Gorakhpur in 1801 brought the two powers into direct contact.
  • Disputes arose over Butwal and Sheoraj.

30.2 Anglo-Nepalese War

  • The conflict developed during the period of Lord Hastings.
  • The British defeated Nepal in the war.
  • Peace was established through the Treaty of Sagauli in 1816.

30.3 Treaty of Sagauli (1816)

  • Nepal ceded Garhwal and Kumaon.
  • Nepal abandoned claims over the Terai.

30.4 Benefits to the British

  • British territorial influence reached the Himalayas.
  • Trade routes towards Central Asia became more accessible.
  • The British acquired locations suitable for hill stations.
  • Shimla, Mussoorie and Nainital became important British hill stations.
  • Gorkhas joined the British Indian Army in large numbers.

31. Anglo-Burmese Relations

−

31.1 Background

  • Burma was an independent country in the early nineteenth century.
  • It wanted to expand westwards.
  • British expansion was influenced by forest resources, market opportunities, competition with French influence and strategic interests.
  • Three Anglo-Burmese Wars ultimately led to the annexation of Burma in 1885.

31.2 First Anglo-Burmese War (1824–1826)

Causes

  • Burmese expansion towards the west.
  • Occupation of Arakan and Manipur.
  • Threat to Assam and the Brahmaputra Valley.
  • Border disputes between British India and Burma.

Course

  • British forces occupied Rangoon in May 1824.
  • They advanced towards Ava, the Burmese capital.
  • Peace was concluded in 1826.

Treaty of Yandabo (1826)

  • Burma agreed to pay ₹1 crore as war compensation.
  • Burma ceded Arakan and Tenasserim.
  • Burma abandoned claims over Assam, Cachar and Jaintia.
  • Manipur was recognised as an independent state.
  • Burma agreed to negotiate a commercial treaty with Britain.
  • A British Resident was accepted at Ava.
  • Burma could post an envoy at Calcutta.

31.3 Second Anglo-Burmese War (1852)

Causes

  • British commercial interests.
  • Desire to control timber resources.
  • Expansionist policy of Lord Dalhousie.
  • Demand for greater access to the Burmese market.

Result

  • The British occupied Pegu. Lower Burma came under British control after resistance.

31.4 Third Anglo-Burmese War (1885)

Causes

  • King Thibaw's hostility towards the British.
  • Commercial disputes involving British timber interests.
  • Burmese negotiations with France, Germany and Italy.
  • British concern over growing French influence.
  • British imperial ambitions.

Result

  • Lord Dufferin ordered the invasion.
  • Upper Burma was annexed in 1885.
  • Burma was brought under British control.

31.5 Later Developments

  • Burmese resistance continued after annexation.
  • Nationalist movements developed during the twentieth century.
  • Burma was separated from India in 1935.
  • Burma achieved independence on 4 January 1948.

32. Anglo-Tibetan Relations

−

32.1 Background

  • Tibet was governed through a Buddhist theocratic system.
  • The Dalai Lama was an important religious and political authority.
  • Tibet was nominally under Chinese suzerainty.
  • British attempts to establish commercial and diplomatic relations had not succeeded.
  • Lord Curzon became concerned about increasing Russian influence in Tibet.

32.2 Younghusband Mission

  • Lord Curzon sent a mission under Colonel Younghusband.
  • The British were concerned about reports of Russian weapons and ammunition reaching Tibet.
  • The Tibetans resisted negotiations.
  • Younghusband advanced towards Lhasa.
  • The Dalai Lama fled.
  • British forces reached Lhasa in August 1904.

32.3 Treaty of Lhasa (1904)

  • Tibet agreed to pay an indemnity of ₹75 lakh.
  • The Chumbi Valley was to remain under British occupation as security for payment.
  • Tibet agreed to respect the Sikkim frontier.
  • Trade marts were to be opened at Yatung, Gyantse and Gartok.
  • Tibet was restricted from granting certain foreign concessions involving railways, roads and telegraphs.
  • Britain gained influence over Tibet's external relations.

32.4 Revised Arrangements

  • The indemnity was later reduced from ₹75 lakh to ₹25 lakh.
  • Chumbi Valley was to be evacuated after three years.
  • It was actually evacuated in January 1908.
  • The Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 restricted direct dealings with Tibet except through China.

33. Anglo-Afghan Relations

−

33.1 Background

  • Russia's increasing influence in Central Asia alarmed the British.
  • The British feared a possible Russian threat to India.
  • Afghanistan was strategically important because of its geographical position.
  • The British wanted Afghanistan to be ruled by a ruler friendly to British India.

33.2 Forward Policy of Lord Auckland

  • Lord Auckland became Governor-General in 1836.
  • He advocated an aggressive Forward Policy.
  • The policy aimed to protect British India from a possible Russian attack.
  • It involved treaties with neighbouring states, military intervention and territorial expansion where considered necessary.

33.3 Dost Mohammed

  • Dost Mohammed was the Amir of Afghanistan.
  • He wanted British friendship.
  • He sought British help to recover Peshawar from the Sikhs.
  • The British rejected this condition.
  • Dost Mohammed turned towards Russia and Persia for assistance.

33.4 Tripartite Treaty (1838)

The agreement involved the British, Sikhs and Shah Shuja.

  • Shah Shuja would be restored to the Afghan throne with military assistance.
  • The British would provide financial support.
  • Shah Shuja would conduct foreign affairs with the advice of the British and Sikhs.
  • He would recognise Ranjit Singh's claims over Afghan territories on the right bank of the Indus.
  • He would surrender certain claims over Sindh in return for financial compensation.

34. First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842)

−

34.1 Causes

  • British fear of Russian influence.
  • British desire to establish a friendly government in Afghanistan.
  • Opposition to Dost Mohammed's foreign relations.
  • Implementation of Lord Auckland's Forward Policy.

34.2 Course

  • British forces entered Kabul in August 1839.
  • Dost Mohammed surrendered in 1840.
  • Shah Shuja was placed on the Afghan throne.
  • Shah Shuja was unpopular among the Afghans.
  • Afghan resistance increased.
  • The British garrison commander in Kabul was killed.
  • The British agreed to evacuate Afghanistan and restore Dost Mohammed.
  • British forces reoccupied Kabul in September 1842.
  • Ultimately, the British recognised Dost Mohammed as the independent ruler of Afghanistan.

34.3 Consequences

  • The British failed to establish a stable government through Shah Shuja.
  • The war cost India approximately ₹1.5 crore.
  • Nearly 20,000 men were lost, according to the chapter.
  • British confidence in aggressive intervention in Afghanistan declined.

35. John Lawrence and the Policy of Masterly Inactivity

−

35.1 Period

  • John Lawrence served as Viceroy from 1864 to 1869.

35.2 Meaning

  • Masterly Inactivity was a policy of avoiding unnecessary intervention in Afghanistan.
  • It developed as a reaction to the disastrous First Anglo-Afghan War.

35.3 Main Features

  • Respect Afghan independence.
  • Avoid unnecessary military intervention.
  • Maintain peaceful frontier relations.
  • Avoid interfering in succession disputes.
  • Establish friendly relations with the ruler who gained power.

35.4 Conditions

  1. Peace along the frontier should not be disturbed.
  2. Afghan candidates involved in civil war should not seek foreign assistance.

35.5 Significance

  • Reduced the danger of expensive military intervention.
  • Respected Afghanistan's political independence.
  • Encouraged peaceful relations with Sher Ali.

36. Lytton and the Policy of Proud Reserve

−

36.1 Background

  • Lord Lytton became Viceroy in 1876.
  • He was appointed by the Conservative government under Benjamin Disraeli.
  • He wanted a more assertive policy towards Afghanistan.

36.2 Meaning

  • Proud Reserve aimed at securing scientific frontiers and safeguarding British spheres of influence.
  • Lytton believed that relations with Afghanistan should not remain ambiguous.

37. Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–1880)

−

37.1 Causes

  • Sher Ali wanted to maintain relations with both Russia and British India.
  • He refused to accept a permanent British envoy at Kabul.
  • He had earlier allowed a Russian envoy.
  • Lord Lytton considered this unacceptable.
  • The British decided to invade Afghanistan.

37.2 Treaty of Gandamak (May 1879)

The treaty was signed with Yakub Khan, the son of Sher Ali.

  • The Amir would conduct foreign policy with the advice of the Government of India.
  • A permanent British Resident would be stationed at Kabul.
  • The British would provide support against foreign aggression.
  • The Amir would receive an annual subsidy.

37.3 Consequences

  • Yakub Khan later abdicated.
  • British forces recaptured Kabul and Kandahar.
  • Abdur Rahman became Amir.
  • Lytton planned to divide Afghanistan, but the plan was not implemented.
  • Lord Ripon abandoned the plan.
  • Afghanistan was maintained as a buffer state.

37.4 Later Developments

  • Following the First World War and Russian Revolution of 1917, Afghanistan demanded complete independence.
  • Amanullah declared war against the British in 1919.
  • Peace was concluded in 1921.
  • Afghanistan recovered independence in foreign affairs.

38. British India and the North-West Frontier

−

38.1 Strategic Importance

  • The region between the Indus River and Afghanistan was important for British security.
  • The British wanted a secure frontier against possible invasions.
  • The conquest of Sindh in 1843 and Punjab in 1849 brought British territory beyond the Indus.
  • The British came into contact with Baluch and Pathan tribes.

38.2 Durand Agreement (1893)

  • British occupation of Hunza and Nagar in the Gilgit Valley created tensions.
  • Amir Abdur Rahman of Afghanistan was concerned.
  • A boundary agreement was negotiated.
  • The boundary became known as the Durand Line.
  • The agreement was concluded in 1893.

38.3 Tribal Resistance

  • The agreement did not establish permanent peace.
  • Tribal uprisings continued.
  • British forces established a garrison at Chitral.
  • Troops were deployed at Malakand Pass.
  • Resistance continued until 1898.

38.4 Lord Curzon's Policy

  • Lord Curzon served as Viceroy from 1899 to 1905.
  • He followed a policy of withdrawal and concentration.
  • British troops were withdrawn from some advanced posts.
  • Tribal levies were trained and commanded by British officers.
  • The British created the North-West Frontier Province under the Government of India.
  • The policy helped establish comparatively peaceful conditions in the region.

42. Important Concepts for Competitive Examinations

−

42.1 Diwani

  • Right to collect land revenue and administer revenue-related affairs.
  • Granted to the East India Company by Shah Alam II in 1765.
  • Covered Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

42.2 Nizamat

  • Administrative functions involving police, military defence and justice.
  • Came under Company influence during the Dual Government system.

42.3 Dastak

  • Trade permit allowing specified goods to move without payment of duties.
  • Misuse by Company servants created conflict with Mir Kasim.

42.4 Subsidiary Alliance

  • Political and military arrangement developed under Lord Wellesley.
  • Indian rulers accepted British troops and paid for their maintenance.
  • Their foreign policy and military independence were restricted.

42.5 Doctrine of Lapse

  • Associated with Lord Dalhousie.
  • Used to annex states where the British refused to recognise adopted heirs as successors to the throne.

42.6 Paramountcy

  • British claim to supreme political authority over Indian princely states.
  • Expanded through conquest, treaties and administrative intervention.

42.7 Buffer State

  • A state situated between two competing powers.
  • Awadh was used as a buffer state in the context of British frontier security.

42.8 Forward Policy

  • Associated with Lord Auckland.
  • Favoured active measures to secure the north-western frontier and counter Russian influence.

42.9 Masterly Inactivity

  • Associated with John Lawrence.
  • Favoured restraint and limited intervention in Afghan affairs.

42.10 Proud Reserve

  • Associated with Lord Lytton.
  • Favoured a more assertive approach towards Afghanistan.

45. Final Chapter Summary

−

The expansion and consolidation of British power in India was a gradual process extending from the eighteenth to the nineteenth century.

Major Stages of British Expansion

1. Commercial Establishment

  • The East India Company initially established trading centres and factories in different parts of India.

2. Political Intervention

  • The Company exploited political rivalries among Indian rulers to increase its influence.

3. Conquest of Bengal

  • The Battles of Plassey and Buxar established British political and financial dominance in Bengal.

4. Expansion in Southern India

  • Four Anglo-Mysore Wars weakened Mysore and ended Tipu Sultan's resistance in 1799.

5. Destruction of Maratha Supremacy

  • Three Anglo-Maratha Wars weakened the Maratha confederacy and ended the Peshwaship.

6. Expansion Towards North-Western India

  • The conquest of Sindh in 1843 and Punjab in 1849 extended British territorial control.

7. Administrative Expansion

  • The Ring-Fence Policy, Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse expanded British paramountcy.

8. Expansion Beyond India

  • British involvement in Nepal, Burma, Tibet, Bhutan and Afghanistan strengthened its strategic position.
Central Conclusion: British power in India was established through a combination of military superiority, financial strength, political diplomacy, exploitation of internal rivalries and administrative policies. By the middle of the nineteenth century, the British had defeated or subordinated most major Indian powers and established their paramountcy over the subcontinent.
