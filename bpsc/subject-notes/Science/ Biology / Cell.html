01. Introduction to Cell Biology

⌄

Cell Biology (Cytology) is the branch of biology that deals with the study of cells, their structure, functions, chemical composition, growth, reproduction and interactions.

Definition: The cell is the basic structural, functional and biological unit of life.
  • All living organisms are made up of one or more cells.
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells.
  • The cell contains hereditary information (DNA).
  • Cells carry out essential life processes such as metabolism, growth and reproduction.

Important Terminology

03. Cell Theory

⌄

Cell theory was developed mainly by Matthias Schleiden and Theodor Schwann.

Three Main Principles

  1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
  3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells.

Modern Cell Theory

  • Cells contain hereditary information in the form of DNA.
  • Energy flow occurs within cells.
  • All cells have a broadly similar chemical composition.
  • The activities of an organism depend on the activities of its cells.

Exceptions and Special Cases

  • Viruses are acellular; they are not made of cells.
  • Mature mammalian red blood cells lack a nucleus.
  • Skeletal muscle fibres are multinucleated.
  • Some fungal and algal structures are coenocytic and contain many nuclei in common cytoplasm.

04. Types of Cells – Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic

⌄

A. Prokaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells do not have a membrane-bound nucleus or membrane-bound organelles.

  • Examples: Bacteria and Archaea.
  • Generally smaller and simpler.
  • Genetic material is present in a nucleoid region.
  • Usually contain circular DNA.
  • Ribosomes are 70S.
  • Cell division generally occurs through binary fission.
  • Many bacteria possess a cell wall.

B. Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cells have a well-defined nucleus enclosed by a nuclear membrane.

  • Examples: Plants, animals, fungi and protists.
  • Generally larger and more complex.
  • Have a membrane-bound nucleus.
  • Possess membrane-bound organelles.
  • Cytoplasmic ribosomes are generally 80S.
  • DNA is arranged into chromosomes.
  • Cell division occurs through mitosis or meiosis.

Difference Between Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain 70S-type ribosomes, even though they occur inside eukaryotic cells.

07. Plasma Membrane (Cell Membrane)

⌄

The plasma membrane is the thin, flexible boundary surrounding the cell.

Structure

  • Made mainly of phospholipids and proteins.
  • Contains cholesterol in animal cells.
  • Has carbohydrate chains attached to lipids and proteins.
  • Described by the fluid mosaic model.

Fluid Mosaic Model

  • Proposed by S. J. Singer and G. L. Nicolson in 1972.
  • Consists of a fluid phospholipid bilayer with proteins embedded in it.
  • Lipids and many proteins can move laterally within the membrane.

Functions

  1. Protects the cell.
  2. Separates internal and external environments.
  3. Controls movement of substances.
  4. Helps cell communication.
  5. Supports cell recognition.
  6. Participates in endocytosis and exocytosis.

Types of Membrane Transport

A. Passive Transport

Movement of substances without direct expenditure of cellular energy.

  • Simple diffusion: Higher to lower concentration.
  • Facilitated diffusion: Through membrane proteins.
  • Osmosis: Movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane.

B. Active Transport

Movement of substances against their concentration or electrochemical gradient using energy.

Example: Sodium-potassium pump.

C. Bulk Transport

  • Endocytosis: Cell takes substances inside by forming vesicles.
  • Exocytosis: Cell releases substances through vesicles.

Osmosis and Its Types

SolutionEffect on Animal Cell
HypotonicWater enters; cell may swell.
HypertonicWater leaves; cell shrinks.
IsotonicNo net movement of water.
Plant cells in a hypotonic solution become turgid because of their rigid cell wall.

08. Cell Wall

⌄

The cell wall is a rigid outer covering found in plants, fungi, bacteria and some other organisms. It is absent in animal cells.

Composition

OrganismMain Cell Wall Material
PlantsCellulose
FungiChitin
BacteriaPeptidoglycan
DiatomsSilica in cell wall
Some algaeCellulose and other substances

Functions

  • Provides shape and mechanical support.
  • Protects the cell.
  • Prevents excessive swelling in hypotonic conditions.
  • Allows movement of water and many small substances through its porous structure.

09. Cytoplasm

⌄

Cytoplasm is the material present between the plasma membrane and the nucleus.

Components

  • Cytosol: Fluid component.
  • Cell organelles: Specialized structures.
  • Cytoskeleton: Protein fibre network.
  • Stored substances and inclusions.

Functions

  • Site of many metabolic reactions.
  • Supports and suspends organelles.
  • Helps intracellular transport.
  • Participates in cell movement and shape maintenance.

10. Nucleus

⌄

The nucleus is generally the largest membrane-bound organelle in a typical eukaryotic cell. It contains most of the cell's genetic material.

Main Parts

  1. Nuclear envelope
  2. Nuclear pores
  3. Nucleoplasm
  4. Chromatin
  5. Nucleolus

Functions

  • Controls and coordinates many cellular activities.
  • Stores genetic information.
  • Regulates gene expression.
  • Participates in cell division.
  • Nucleolus helps produce ribosomal RNA and assemble ribosomal subunits.
The nucleus was discovered by Robert Brown in 1831. Mature mammalian RBCs lack a nucleus. Prokaryotes do not have a membrane-bound nucleus.

11. Cell Organelles – Complete Details

⌄

A. Mitochondria

Mitochondria are double-membrane-bound organelles responsible for most aerobic ATP production in eukaryotic cells.

Structure

  • Outer membrane
  • Inner membrane
  • Cristae
  • Matrix
  • Intermembrane space
  • Mitochondrial DNA
  • Ribosomes

Functions

  1. ATP production through cellular respiration.
  2. Role in energy metabolism.
  3. Participation in programmed cell death (apoptosis).
  4. Involvement in calcium regulation.
Known as the powerhouse of the cell. Mitochondria have their own DNA and ribosomes. Their inner membrane forms folds called cristae.

B. Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER)

The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membrane-bound tubes and sacs.

1. Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (RER)

  • Ribosomes are attached to its surface.
  • Synthesizes proteins for secretion, membranes and certain organelles.
  • Helps in protein folding and processing.

2. Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (SER)

  • Ribosomes are absent on its surface.
  • Synthesizes lipids and steroids.
  • Helps in detoxification.
  • Stores calcium ions, especially in muscle cells.

C. Golgi Apparatus

Golgi apparatus was discovered by Camillo Golgi in 1898.

Structure

  • Consists of flattened membrane-bound sacs called cisternae.
  • Cis face is the receiving side.
  • Trans face is the shipping side.

Functions

  1. Modifies proteins and lipids.
  2. Sorts and packages substances.
  3. Forms secretory vesicles.
  4. Helps in lysosome formation.
  5. Plays an important role in cellular secretion.

D. Lysosomes

Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles containing digestive enzymes.

Functions

  • Break down waste materials.
  • Digest damaged organelles.
  • Destroy certain invading microorganisms.
  • Recycle cellular components.
Lysosomes are sometimes called the digestive bags or suicidal bags of the cell.

E. Ribosomes

Ribosomes are non-membranous structures responsible for protein synthesis.

RibosomeSubunits
70S50S + 30S
80S60S + 40S
  • Found free in cytoplasm.
  • Attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum.
  • Present in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
  • Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins.

F. Plastids

Plastids are found in plants and many algae.

TypeFunction
ChloroplastPhotosynthesis.
ChromoplastProvides colour to flowers and fruits.
LeucoplastStorage of food.

Types of Leucoplasts

  • Amyloplast: Stores starch.
  • Elaioplast: Stores oils and fats.
  • Proteinoplast: Stores proteins.

G. Chloroplast

Chloroplasts are green plastids containing chlorophyll.

Structure

  • Outer membrane and inner membrane.
  • Stroma.
  • Thylakoids.
  • Grana.
  • Stroma lamellae.
  • Chloroplast DNA and ribosomes.

Functions

  • Absorb light energy.
  • Carry out photosynthesis.
  • Produce carbohydrates using light energy.
  • Release oxygen during oxygenic photosynthesis.
Grana are stacks of thylakoids. Light-dependent reactions occur in thylakoid membranes. The Calvin cycle occurs in the stroma.

H. Vacuoles

Vacuoles are membrane-bound compartments containing water and dissolved substances.

  • Store water, ions, pigments and waste materials.
  • Maintain turgor pressure in plant cells.
  • Help in osmoregulation.
  • Assist in intracellular digestion in some organisms.
The membrane surrounding a plant vacuole is called the tonoplast.

I. Centrosome and Centrioles

  • Centrosome is an important microtubule-organizing centre in many animal cells.
  • It usually contains a pair of centrioles.
  • Centrioles help organize the spindle apparatus during cell division.
  • Centrioles are generally absent in higher plant cells.

J. Peroxisomes

  • Small membrane-bound organelles containing oxidative enzymes.
  • Break down fatty acids.
  • Help detoxify harmful substances.
  • Break down hydrogen peroxide using catalase.
  • Participate in certain metabolic pathways.

K. Cytoskeleton

The cytoskeleton is a network of protein fibres that provides structural support.

ComponentMain Function
MicrofilamentsCell movement and contraction.
Intermediate filamentsMechanical strength.
MicrotubulesIntracellular transport and spindle formation.

14. Chromosomes and Genes

⌄

Chromosome: A DNA-protein structure that carries genetic information.

Gene: A segment of DNA that contributes to a functional product, such as a protein or functional RNA.

Important Terms

  • DNA: Deoxyribonucleic acid.
  • RNA: Ribonucleic acid.
  • Chromatin: DNA associated with proteins.
  • Chromatid: One of the two copies of a replicated chromosome.
  • Centromere: Region joining sister chromatids.
  • Telomere: Protective region at chromosome ends.
  • Histones: Proteins around which DNA is packaged.

15. Cell Division – Mitosis and Meiosis

⌄

Cell division is the process through which a parent cell divides to form daughter cells.

A. Mitosis

Mitosis is a type of cell division in which one parent cell generally produces two genetically similar daughter cells with the same chromosome number.

Stages of Mitosis

Importance of Mitosis

  • Growth of organisms.
  • Repair of damaged tissues.
  • Replacement of old cells.
  • Asexual reproduction in many organisms.

B. Meiosis

Meiosis is a specialized type of cell division that reduces the chromosome number by half and produces haploid cells.

It consists of two successive divisions:

  • Meiosis I: Reductional division.
  • Meiosis II: Equational division.

Meiosis I Stages

  1. Prophase I
  2. Metaphase I
  3. Anaphase I
  4. Telophase I

Importance of Meiosis

  • Formation of haploid gametes in animals.
  • Maintenance of chromosome number across generations after fertilization.
  • Genetic variation through crossing over and independent assortment.

16. Cell Cycle

⌄

The cell cycle is the sequence of events through which a cell grows, duplicates its DNA and divides.

  • G1 phase: Cell growth and normal activity.

22. Cell Death – Apoptosis and Necrosis

⌄

A. Apoptosis

  • Programmed cell death.
  • Usually controlled and regulated.
  • Helps remove unwanted or damaged cells.
  • Important in development and tissue maintenance.

B. Necrosis

  • Uncontrolled cell death caused by injury or severe damage.
  • Often associated with inflammation.
