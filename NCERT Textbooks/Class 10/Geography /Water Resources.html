Chapter Introduction

We share this planet with millions of other living beings, starting from micro-organisms and bacteria to lichens, banyan trees, elephants and blue whales.

All living organisms form a complex ecological system. Human beings are only one part of this system and depend on it for their existence.

Forests play a key role in the ecological system because they are primary producers on which other living beings depend.

Chapter Focus: Biodiversity, flora and fauna, conservation of forests and wildlife, Project Tiger, forest classification, community participation, Sacred Groves and sustainable conservation.
1. INTRODUCTION TO BIODIVERSITY AND ECOLOGICAL SYSTEM

1.1 Our Planet and Living Organisms

Our planet contains millions of living organisms. These include micro-organisms, bacteria, lichens, plants, animals, elephants and blue whales.

  • All living organisms are interconnected.
  • Human beings are dependent on the ecological system.
  • Plants, animals and micro-organisms help maintain the quality of air.
  • They contribute to the quality of water.
  • They help maintain the soil that produces food.
  • Forests are primary producers in the ecological system.

1.2 Meaning of Biodiversity

Biodiversity or Biological Diversity refers to the immense variety of wildlife and cultivated species, diverse in form and function, but closely integrated through multiple networks of interdependence.

1.4 Flora and Fauna in India

  • India is one of the world's richest countries in biological diversity.
  • Different regions have unique plants and animals.
  • Many species are yet to be discovered.
  • Flora and fauna are integrated into our daily life.
  • These resources are under great stress due to insensitivity towards the environment.

1.5 Ecological Importance of Forests

  • Forests are primary producers.
  • They support other living beings.
  • They help maintain ecological balance.
  • They provide habitat for wildlife.
  • They contribute to the maintenance of air, water and soil.
Important Definition:
Biodiversity is the variety of living organisms, including plants, animals and micro-organisms, and their interdependence within an ecological system.

1.6 Lepcha Folk Song and Nature

The chapter refers to a Lepcha folk song addressed to Narak. It describes music gathered from springs, rivers, mountains, forests, insects, animals and the sweet breeze.

The song reflects the harmonious relationship between human beings and nature.

2. CONSERVATION OF FOREST AND WILDLIFE IN INDIA

2.1 Meaning of Conservation

Conservation means protecting and carefully managing forests, wildlife and natural resources to prevent their destruction and maintain ecological balance.

2.2 Why Do We Need to Conserve Forests and Wildlife?

Conservation has become essential because of the rapid decline in wildlife populations and forestry.

Major Reasons

  1. Preservation of Ecological Diversity: Conservation protects the variety of living organisms.
  2. Protection of Life-Support Systems: It preserves water, air and soil.
  3. Protection of Genetic Diversity: It preserves the genetic diversity of plants and animals.
  4. Growth and Breeding: It supports better growth and breeding of species.
  5. Agricultural Importance: Agriculture continues to depend on traditional crop varieties.
  6. Aquatic Biodiversity: Fisheries depend on the maintenance of aquatic biodiversity.

2.4 Wildlife Protection Programme

  • During the 1960s and 1970s, conservationists demanded a national wildlife protection programme.
  • The Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act was implemented in 1972.
  • The Act included provisions for protecting wildlife habitats.
  • An all-India list of protected species was published.

2.5 Main Objectives of Wildlife Protection Programme

  1. Protect the remaining populations of endangered species.
  2. Ban hunting of protected species.
  3. Provide legal protection to wildlife habitats.
  4. Restrict trade in wildlife.
  5. Establish national parks and wildlife sanctuaries.
Important Year: 1972
Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act was implemented in 1972.

Central and many State Governments established national parks and wildlife sanctuaries for protecting wildlife and their habitats.

3. PROJECT TIGER

3.1 Introduction

  • Tiger is one of the key wildlife species in the faunal web.
  • Project Tiger was launched in 1973.
  • It is one of the well-publicised wildlife campaigns in the world.

3.2 Decline in Tiger Population

Period Tiger Population
Turn of the 20th Century Estimated 55,000
1973 1,827

3.3 Major Threats to Tiger Population

  1. Poaching for trade.
  2. Shrinking natural habitat.
  3. Depletion of prey-base species.
  4. Growing human population.
  5. Trade in tiger skins.
  6. Use of tiger bones in traditional medicines, especially in Asian countries.
  7. Illegal wildlife trade.

3.4 India and Nepal's Importance

  • India and Nepal provide habitat to about two-thirds of the surviving tiger population in the world, according to NCERT.
  • These countries became major targets for poaching and illegal trade.

3.5 Objectives of Project Tiger

  • Protect tigers from extinction.
  • Conserve their natural habitats.
  • Protect prey species.
  • Preserve larger natural ecosystems.
  • Maintain biodiversity.

3.7 Expansion of Conservation Efforts

  • Conservation projects gradually focused on biodiversity rather than only a few components.
  • Insects began receiving attention in conservation planning.
  • Notifications under the Wildlife Act of 1980 and 1986 added several hundred butterflies, moths, beetles and one dragonfly to the protected list.
  • In 1991, plants were included in the protected list for the first time, beginning with six species.

4. TYPES AND DISTRIBUTION OF FOREST RESOURCES

4.1 Government Management of Forests

Much of India's forest and wildlife resources are owned or managed by the government through the Forest Department or other government departments.

These forests are classified into three categories:

FOREST RESOURCES IN INDIA
Reserved Forests
More than half of total forest land
Protected Forests
Almost one-third of total forest area
Unclassed Forests
Other forests and wastelands

4.2 Reserved Forests

  • More than half of the total forest land has been declared reserved forests.
  • They are regarded as the most valuable for conservation of forest and wildlife resources.

4.3 Protected Forests

  • Almost one-third of the total forest area is protected forest.
  • These forests are declared by the Forest Department.
  • They are protected from further depletion.

4.4 Unclassed Forests

  • These are other forests and wastelands.
  • They may belong to the government.
  • They may belong to private individuals.
  • They may belong to communities.

4.5 Permanent Forest Estates

Reserved and protected forests are also referred to as permanent forest estates.

They are maintained for:

  1. Producing timber.
  2. Producing other forest products.
  3. Protective purposes.

5. COMMUNITY AND CONSERVATION

5.1 Role of Local Communities

Conservation strategies are not new in India. Forests are also home to traditional communities. Local people often depend on forests for their livelihoods.

  • Communities work with government officials to protect habitats.
  • Some villagers independently protect forests.
  • They recognise that conservation secures their long-term livelihood.
  • Traditional knowledge helps protect biodiversity.

5.2 Sariska Tiger Reserve – Rajasthan

  • Villagers in Sariska Tiger Reserve struggled against mining activities.
  • They cited the Wildlife Protection Act.
  • This demonstrates the importance of local community participation.

5.3 Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' – Alwar, Rajasthan

  • Five villages in Alwar district declared 1,200 hectares of forest as Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri'.
  • Villagers established their own rules and regulations.
  • Hunting was prohibited.
  • Wildlife was protected against outside encroachments.
Important Exam Fact:
Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' – Alwar, Rajasthan – Five villages – 1,200 hectares of forest.

5.4 Chipko Movement

  • The Chipko Movement took place in the Himalayas.
  • It successfully resisted deforestation in several areas.
  • It demonstrated the effectiveness of community afforestation.
  • It showed the importance of planting indigenous species.

5.5 Beej Bachao Andolan

  • Associated with Tehri.
  • Farmers and citizens' groups participated.
  • It demonstrated that diversified crop production is possible without synthetic chemicals.
  • Such practices can be economically viable.

5.6 Navdanya

  • Mentioned along with Beej Bachao Andolan.
  • Demonstrated the importance of diversified crop production.
  • Supported farming without synthetic chemicals.

5.7 Joint Forest Management (JFM)

Meaning

Joint Forest Management is a programme that involves local communities in the management and restoration of degraded forests.

Origin

  • The programme has been in formal existence since 1988.
  • Odisha passed the first resolution for Joint Forest Management.

Working of JFM

  1. Local village institutions are formed.
  2. These institutions undertake protection activities.
  3. Activities mainly take place on degraded forest land managed by the Forest Department.
  4. Communities help protect and restore forests.
  5. Community members receive benefits in return.

Benefits to Local Communities

  • Non-timber forest produce.
  • A share in the timber harvested through successful protection.

5.9 Importance of Community Participation

  • Local communities should be involved in natural resource management.
  • They have traditional knowledge of their environment.
  • Their livelihoods often depend on forests.
  • Community participation improves conservation efforts.
  • Development activities should be people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding.
6. SACRED GROVES – A WEALTH OF DIVERSE AND RARE SPECIES

6.1 Meaning of Sacred Groves

Sacred Groves are patches of forests or parts of large forests that have been preserved in their natural form due to traditional religious and cultural beliefs.

They are also called:

  • Forests of Gods.
  • Forests of Goddesses.

6.2 Main Features

  1. Based on traditional nature worship.
  2. Local people believe that all creations of nature should be protected.
  3. Forest patches are left untouched.
  4. Interference with these forests is banned.
  5. They help preserve diverse and rare species.

6.5 Protection of Animals through Traditional Beliefs

  • Springs, mountain peaks, plants and animals are considered sacred in several cultures.
  • Macaques and langurs are found around many temples.
  • They are fed daily and treated as part of temple communities.
  • In Bishnoi villages of Rajasthan, wildlife is protected as an integral part of community life.

6.6 Animals Protected by Bishnoi Communities

  • Blackbuck.
  • Chinkara.
  • Nilgai.
  • Peacocks.

6.7 Importance of Sacred Groves

  • Preserve rare species.
  • Protect natural vegetation.
  • Maintain biodiversity.
  • Encourage traditional conservation practices.
  • Promote harmony between human beings and nature.

10. NCERT EXERCISE – SOLVED ANSWERS

A. Multiple Choice Question

Q1. Which of the following conservation strategies do not directly involve community participation?

(a) Joint Forest Management
(b) Beej Bachao Andolan
(c) Chipko Movement
(d) Demarcation of Wildlife Sanctuaries

Correct Answer: (d) Demarcation of Wildlife Sanctuaries

Explanation: Joint Forest Management, Beej Bachao Andolan and Chipko Movement directly involve community participation. Demarcation of wildlife sanctuaries is a formal protection measure.

C. Answer in About 30 Words

Q1. What is biodiversity? Why is biodiversity important for human lives?
Biodiversity refers to the variety of plants, animals and micro-organisms. It maintains ecological balance and supports essential life-support systems such as air, water and soil. It also preserves genetic diversity.
Q2. How have human activities affected the depletion of flora and fauna? Explain.
Human activities such as deforestation, mining, habitat destruction, poaching, hunting and illegal wildlife trade have caused the depletion of flora and fauna. These activities disturb ecological balance and threaten endangered species.

D. Answer in About 120 Words

Q1. Describe how communities have conserved and protected forests and wildlife in India.
Local communities have played an important role in conserving forests and wildlife in India.
  1. In Sariska Tiger Reserve, Rajasthan, villagers opposed mining by citing the Wildlife Protection Act.
  2. Five villages in Alwar district declared 1,200 hectares of forest as Bhairodev Dakav 'Sonchuri' and prohibited hunting.
  3. The Chipko Movement in the Himalayas successfully resisted deforestation and promoted community afforestation.
  4. Beej Bachao Andolan in Tehri and Navdanya demonstrated the possibility of diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals.
  5. Sacred Groves preserved rare plant and animal species through traditional religious beliefs.
  6. Joint Forest Management, formally existing since 1988, involved local communities in protecting degraded forests.
These examples demonstrate that community participation is essential for successful forest and wildlife conservation.
Q2. Write a note on good practices towards conserving forest and wildlife.
Good practices for conserving forests and wildlife include:
  1. Protecting natural habitats and preventing deforestation.
  2. Preventing hunting, poaching and illegal wildlife trade.
  3. Establishing national parks and wildlife sanctuaries.
  4. Promoting afforestation using indigenous species.
  5. Encouraging community participation in forest management.
  6. Supporting traditional conservation practices such as Sacred Groves.
  7. Promoting movements such as Chipko.
  8. Encouraging diversified crop production without synthetic chemicals.
  9. Implementing Joint Forest Management.
  10. Protecting endangered species through legal measures.
Conservation should involve local communities and promote development that is people-centric, environment-friendly and economically rewarding.
