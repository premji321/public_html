1. Introduction to Water Resources

1.1 Distribution of Water on Earth

  • Three-fourths of the Earth's surface is covered with water.
  • Only a small proportion accounts for freshwater that can be put to use.
  • Freshwater is mainly obtained from surface run-off and groundwater, and is continually renewed and recharged through the hydrological cycle.

1.2 Water as a Renewable Resource

Water is a renewable resource because it continuously moves through the hydrological cycle, which renews and recharges freshwater resources.

Main processes of the hydrological cycle

  1. Evaporation: Conversion of water into water vapour due to heat.
  2. Condensation: Water vapour cools and forms tiny droplets or clouds.
  3. Precipitation: Water falls as rain, snow or hail.
  4. Surface run-off: Water flows over land towards rivers, lakes and oceans.
  5. Infiltration: Water enters the soil.
  6. Groundwater recharge: Water replenishes underground reserves.
Important: Water is renewable, but usable freshwater is limited and unevenly distributed.

2. Water Scarcity and Its Causes

2.1 Meaning of Water Scarcity

Water scarcity is a situation in which sufficient usable water is not available to meet the requirements of people, agriculture, industries and other activities.

2.2 Natural Causes

  1. Unequal distribution of rainfall.
  2. Seasonal and annual variations in precipitation.
  3. Low annual rainfall.
  4. Drought conditions.
  5. Regional variations in water availability.

2.3 Human-Induced Causes

A. Over-exploitation of water resources

  • Excessive extraction of groundwater.
  • Uncontrolled use of water for irrigation.
  • Continuous pumping through wells and tube-wells.
  • Declining groundwater levels.

B. Population growth

  • Increasing demand for drinking water and domestic use.
  • Greater demand for food production.
  • Higher pressure on available freshwater resources.

C. Agricultural activities

  • Irrigated agriculture is the largest consumer of water.
  • Expansion of irrigated areas increases water demand.
  • Excessive groundwater extraction can affect water availability and food security.
  • Water-intensive crops increase pressure on resources.

D. Industrialisation

  • Industries consume large quantities of freshwater.
  • Industrial activities also require power; much energy comes from hydroelectric power.
  • Industrial wastes can pollute freshwater resources.

E. Urbanisation

  • Growing urban centres and dense populations increase water and energy requirements.
  • Housing colonies often use groundwater-pumping devices.
  • Urban lifestyles add pressure to fragile water resources.

F. Unequal access and water pollution

  • Water is not equally accessible to all social groups.
  • Domestic and industrial wastes, chemicals, pesticides and fertilisers can make water hazardous for human use.

2.4 Why Can Scarcity Occur in High-Rainfall Areas?

Water scarcity can occur even where water resources are ample because of large and growing populations, greater demand, over-exploitation, unequal access and poor water quality.

Exam point: Water scarcity is not caused only by low rainfall. Pollution can create scarcity of usable water even where water is available.

2.5 Consequences

  • Falling groundwater levels.
  • Reduced drinking-water availability.
  • Threats to food security.
  • Health hazards.
  • Agricultural and industrial difficulties.
  • Degradation of natural ecosystems.

Water resources must be conserved and managed to safeguard people from health hazards, ensure food security, support livelihoods and productive activities, and prevent degradation of natural ecosystems.

4. Government Initiatives for Water Conservation

4.1 Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM)

Goal: Enable every rural household to get an assured supply of potable piped water at a service level of 55 litres per capita per day (LPCD) on a regular, long-term basis by ensuring functionality of tap-water connections.

  • Focuses on rural households.
  • Assured supply of potable piped water.
  • Service level: 55 litres per capita per day.
  • Emphasises regular and long-term supply and functional tap connections.

NCERT source note: Economic Survey 2020–21, page 357.

4.2 Atal Bhujal Yojana (Atal Jal)

Objective: Promote sustainable groundwater management and bring behavioural changes in communities, shifting from consumption towards conservation and smart water management.

8,220Water-stressed Gram Panchayats

Seven participating states: Gujarat, Haryana, Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Rajasthan and Uttar Pradesh.

  • The selected states account for about 37% of India's water-stressed blocks.
  • Water-stressed blocks include over-exploited, critical and semi-critical blocks.

NCERT source note: Annual Report, Ministry of Jal Shakti, Government of India, 2022–23.

4.3 Pradhan Mantri Krishi Sinchayee Yojana (PMKSY)

Objective: Expand irrigation access, improve on-farm water-use efficiency and promote sustainable water conservation.

  • Expand cultivable area under assured irrigation – Har Khet Ko Pani.
  • Improve on-farm water-use efficiency and reduce wastage.
  • Increase availability of irrigation water in duration and extent.
  • Promote irrigation and other water-saving technologies – Per Drop More Crop.
  • Introduce sustainable water-conservation practices.

6. Dams and Multi-purpose River Projects

6.1 Meaning of a Dam

A dam is a barrier across flowing water that obstructs, directs or retards the flow, often creating a reservoir, lake or impoundment.

  • Reservoir: Storage area where water is collected behind a dam.
  • Spillway / Weir: Section over or through which water is intended to flow intermittently or continuously.
  • Dams may be classified according to structure, intended purpose or height.

6.2 Classification of Dams

BasisCategories mentioned in NCERT
Structure and materialsTimber dams, embankment dams and masonry dams.
HeightLow dams, medium-height dams and high dams; the text also refers to large and major dams.

6.3 Meaning of Multi-purpose River Projects

Modern dams are built not just for irrigation but for several purposes. Projects in which different uses of impounded water are integrated are called multi-purpose projects.

6.4 Major Functions

  • Irrigation.
  • Generation of hydroelectricity.
  • Domestic water supply.
  • Industrial water supply.
  • Flood control.
  • Recreation.
  • Inland navigation.
  • Fish breeding.

6.6 Dams – The Temples of Modern India

After Independence, multi-purpose projects were considered vehicles of national development and progress. Jawaharlal Nehru proudly described dams as the “Temples of Modern India”.

They were expected to integrate agricultural development and the village economy with rapid industrialisation and urban economic growth.

7. Disadvantages and Criticism of Multi-purpose Projects

In recent years, multi-purpose projects and large dams have come under scrutiny and opposition because of their social, economic and environmental consequences.

7.1 Environmental Problems

  1. Changes in natural river flow: Regulating and damming rivers affect natural flow and sediment movement.
  2. Sedimentation: Excessive sedimentation at the bottom of reservoirs can produce rockier stream beds and poorer habitats for aquatic life.
  3. Fragmentation of rivers: Dams make it difficult for aquatic fauna to migrate, especially for spawning.
  4. Submergence: Reservoirs on floodplains submerge existing vegetation and soil; submerged vegetation decomposes over time.
  5. Flooding: Sedimentation can reduce reservoir capacity. Large dams may fail to control floods during excessive rainfall and can contribute to flood problems.
  6. Soil erosion and land degradation: Floods damage land, while floodplains may be deprived of silt, a natural fertiliser.
  7. Other ecological effects: Projects may induce earthquakes and contribute to water-borne diseases, pests and pollution associated with excessive use of water.

7.2 Social and Economic Problems

  • Large-scale displacement of people.
  • Loss of livelihood.
  • Submergence of villages and agricultural land.
  • Disruption of traditional economic activities.
  • Unequal distribution of project benefits.

7.3 Changes in Cropping Patterns

Irrigation may encourage farmers to shift towards water-intensive and commercial crops. One ecological consequence is soil salinisation.

10. Rainwater Harvesting

10.1 Meaning

Rainwater harvesting is the process of collecting and storing rainwater for future use or directing it to recharge groundwater.

10.2 Why Was It Considered an Alternative?

With the disadvantages of multi-purpose projects and growing resistance against them, water harvesting was considered a viable alternative from socio-economic and environmental perspectives.

10.3 Traditional Knowledge

People in ancient India understood rainfall regimes and soil types and developed techniques to harvest rainwater, groundwater, river water and floodwater according to local ecological conditions and water needs.

10.4 Importance

  • Conserves rainwater.
  • Stores water for later use.
  • Can recharge groundwater.
  • Provides water during dry periods.
  • Supports local agriculture and domestic needs.

11.1 Guls or Kuls – Western Himalayas

  • These are diversion channels.
  • They channel water for agricultural use.

11.2 Inundation Channels – Bengal

  • Developed in the floodplains of Bengal.
  • Carry floodwater to irrigate agricultural fields.

11.3 Khadins and Johads – Rajasthan

  • In arid and semi-arid regions, fields were converted into rain-fed storage structures.
  • Khadins are associated with Jaisalmer.
  • Johads are found in other parts of Rajasthan.
  • These structures allow water to stand and moisten the soil.

11.4 Tankas – Western Rajasthan

In Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, houses traditionally had underground tanks called tankas for storing drinking water.

  • Tankas could be as large as a big room.
  • They were built inside the main house or courtyard.
  • Sloping rooftops were connected to the tanks through pipes.
  • Rainwater flowed down the pipes and was stored underground.
  • The first spell of rain was usually not collected because it cleaned roofs and pipes.
  • Subsequent showers were collected.

Palar Pani: Rainwater is commonly referred to as Palar Pani in these parts and is considered the purest form of natural water.

Tankas provided a reliable source of drinking water until the next rainfall, particularly in summer when other sources dried up. Some houses had underground rooms adjoining the tankas to keep cool during summer.

12. Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting – Working Process

12.1 Collection and Storage

  1. Rainwater falls on the rooftop.
  2. PVC pipes collect the rainwater.
  3. Water is filtered using sand and bricks.
  4. An underground pipe carries water to a sump for immediate use.
  5. Excess water from the sump is directed to a well.
  6. Water from the well recharges underground reserves.
  7. Groundwater can be drawn from the well later.

12.2 Recharge Methods Shown in NCERT

  • Recharge through a hand pump.
  • Recharge through an abandoned dugwell.

12.3 First Rainfall

The first spell of rain is usually not collected because it cleans the roofs and pipes. Rainwater from subsequent showers is collected.

Remember the sequence: Rooftop → PVC pipe → Filter (sand and bricks) → Sump → Use / Well → Groundwater recharge.

13. Modern Adaptations and Case Studies

13.1 Tamil Nadu – Compulsory Rooftop Rainwater Harvesting

  • Tamil Nadu is the first state in India to make rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory for all houses across the state.
  • Legal provisions exist to punish defaulters.
Question: Which state first made rooftop rainwater harvesting compulsory for all houses? Answer: Tamil Nadu.

13.2 Shillong – Meghalaya

  • Rooftop rainwater harvesting is a common practice in Shillong.
  • Cherrapunjee and Mawsynram, about 55 km from Shillong, receive very high rainfall, yet Shillong faces acute water shortage.
  • Nearly every household in the city has a rooftop harvesting structure.
  • About 15–25% of household water requirements come from rooftop water harvesting.

13.3 Gendathur – Karnataka

  • Gendathur is a remote village in Mysuru district.
  • Nearly 200 households installed rooftop rainwater harvesting systems.
  • The village receives annual precipitation of about 1,000 mm.
  • The textbook gives a collection efficiency of 80% and about 10 fillings.
  • It states that every house can collect and use about 50,000 litres annually.
  • The textbook also states a net annual total of 1,00,000 litres from 200 houses; this aggregate does not arithmetically match the stated per-house figure, so both figures are reproduced as printed.

13.4 Western Rajasthan and Indira Gandhi Canal

  • Rooftop rainwater harvesting has declined in some parts of western Rajasthan because water is available through the perennial Indira Gandhi Canal.
  • Some houses still maintain tankas because residents prefer the taste of stored rainwater to tap water.

14. Bamboo Drip Irrigation System – Meghalaya

Meghalaya has a roughly 200-year-old traditional system of tapping stream and spring water using bamboo pipes.

14.1 Important Facts

Method of movementGravity

14.2 Working Mechanism

  1. Bamboo pipes divert perennial springs on hilltops to lower reaches by gravity.
  2. Water travels over hundreds of metres.
  3. Channel sections distribute water into smaller branches.
  4. Water flow is controlled by changing pipe positions.
  5. Reduced channel sections and diversion units are used at the last stage.
  6. The final channel delivers water near the roots of plants.
Importance: It is a locally adapted, gravity-based irrigation method using bamboo channels to deliver water near plant roots.

Answer: “Multi-purpose projects lead to large-scale displacement and loss of livelihood.” This is a criticism, not an argument in their favour.

19.4 Short Answer Questions – About 30 Words

Q1. Explain how water becomes a renewable resource.
Water is renewable because it continuously circulates through the hydrological cycle. Evaporation, condensation, precipitation, surface run-off and groundwater recharge renew freshwater resources.

Q2. What is water scarcity and what are its main causes?
Water scarcity is a shortage of usable water compared with demand. Its causes include over-exploitation, excessive use, population growth, unequal access, industrialisation, urbanisation and pollution.

Q3. Compare the advantages and disadvantages of multi-purpose river projects.
They provide irrigation, electricity, domestic and industrial water supply and flood control. However, they may cause displacement, sedimentation, ecosystem damage, soil degradation and changes in river flow.

19.5 Long Answer Questions – About 120 Words

Q1. Discuss how rainwater harvesting in semi-arid regions of Rajasthan is carried out.

In the semi-arid and arid regions of Rajasthan, especially Bikaner, Phalodi and Barmer, traditional rooftop rainwater harvesting is practised through underground tanks called tankas. These tanks are constructed inside houses or courtyards and connected to sloping rooftops through pipes. Rainwater falling on rooftops flows through these pipes and is stored in the underground tank. The first spell of rainfall is generally not collected because it cleans the rooftops and pipes. Subsequent rainfall is stored for drinking purposes. The stored water, locally known as Palar Pani, remains available until the next rainfall and provides a reliable source of drinking water, particularly during summer when other sources dry up.

Q2. Describe how modern adaptations of traditional rainwater harvesting methods are being carried out to conserve and store water.

Traditional rainwater harvesting methods are being adapted in different parts of India. In Tamil Nadu, rooftop rainwater harvesting has been made compulsory for houses. In Shillong, nearly every household has a rooftop harvesting structure, meeting around 15–25% of household water requirements. In Gendathur village of Karnataka, nearly 200 households have installed rooftop harvesting systems. Modern systems collect rooftop rainwater through pipes, filter it using sand and bricks, and transfer it to storage tanks or wells. Excess water is used for groundwater recharge. These methods help conserve rainwater, improve groundwater availability and reduce dependence on other water sources.
