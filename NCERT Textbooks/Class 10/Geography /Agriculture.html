Chapter Overview

Agriculture is the main source of livelihood for a large section of India's population. It is a primary economic activity and supplies food as well as raw materials to various industries.

This chapter explains types of farming, cropping seasons, major food crops, non-food crops, geographical conditions and agricultural reforms in India.

1. Introduction to Agriculture

Meaning and Importance

Agriculture is a primary economic activity involving the cultivation of crops and rearing of animals for food and other useful products.

Important Features

  • Approximately two-thirds of India's population is engaged in agricultural activities, as stated in the textbook.
  • Agriculture is a primary economic activity.
  • It produces most of the food that we consume.
  • It supplies raw materials to various industries.
  • Agricultural products such as tea, coffee and spices are exported.
  • Agriculture is an important source of livelihood in India.

2. Types of Farming

Agricultural practices in India vary according to physical environment, technological knowledge and socio-cultural practices.

Major Types of Farming

  • Primitive Subsistence Farming
  • Intensive Subsistence Farming
  • Commercial Farming
  • Plantation Agriculture

2.1 Primitive Subsistence Farming

Primitive subsistence farming is a traditional form of agriculture practised on small patches of land using primitive tools and family or community labour.

Main Characteristics

  • Practised in small patches of land.
  • Uses primitive tools such as hoe, dao and digging sticks.
  • Depends on monsoon rainfall.
  • Depends on natural fertility of the soil.
  • Uses family and community labour.
  • Production is mainly for family consumption.
  • Farmers generally do not use modern agricultural inputs.

Slash and Burn Agriculture

Slash and burn agriculture is a form of shifting cultivation.

Process

  1. Farmers clear a patch of forest land.
  2. Trees and vegetation are cut and burnt.
  3. The cleared land is used for cultivation.
  4. Cereals and other food crops are grown.
  5. When soil fertility decreases, farmers shift to another patch.
  6. The abandoned land gets an opportunity to regain fertility through natural processes.

2.2 Intensive Subsistence Farming

Intensive subsistence farming is practised in areas where there is high population pressure on agricultural land.

Characteristics

  • Practised in densely populated regions.
  • It is labour-intensive agriculture.
  • High doses of biochemical inputs are used.
  • Irrigation is used to increase production.
  • Farmers try to obtain maximum output from limited land.
  • Landholdings become smaller due to inheritance and division among successive generations.
  • There is enormous pressure on agricultural land.
  • Farmers continue cultivation because alternative sources of livelihood may be limited.
Main Objective: To obtain maximum agricultural production from a small area of land.

2.3 Commercial Farming

Commercial farming is an agricultural system in which crops are grown mainly for sale in the market.

Characteristics

  • Uses modern agricultural inputs.
  • Uses High Yielding Variety (HYV) seeds.
  • Uses chemical fertilisers.
  • Uses insecticides and pesticides.
  • Uses modern irrigation facilities.
  • Aims to achieve higher productivity.
  • Production is mainly intended for the market.

Examples

Rice is a commercial crop in Haryana and Punjab, whereas it is mainly a subsistence crop in Odisha, according to the NCERT example.

2.4 Plantation Agriculture

Plantation agriculture is a type of commercial farming in which a single crop is grown over a large area.

Characteristics

  • A single crop is grown over a large area.
  • Plantation farms cover large tracts of land.
  • They use capital-intensive inputs.
  • Migrant labourers are employed.
  • Agriculture and industry are closely connected.
  • The entire produce is used as raw material in respective industries.
  • Transport and communication networks are essential.
  • Processing industries and markets are connected to plantation areas.

Major Plantation Crops

  • Tea
  • Coffee
  • Rubber
  • Sugarcane
  • Banana

Important Examples

  • Tea – Assam and North Bengal.
  • Coffee – Karnataka.

3. Cropping Pattern in India

India has diverse physical and cultural conditions. These differences are reflected in its agricultural practices and cropping patterns.

Three Major Cropping Seasons

  • Rabi
  • Kharif
  • Zaid

3.1 Rabi Season

Sowing Period: October to December.

Harvesting Period: April to June.

Major Rabi Crops

  • Wheat
  • Barley
  • Peas
  • Gram
  • Mustard

Major Producing Regions

  • Punjab
  • Haryana
  • Himachal Pradesh
  • Jammu and Kashmir
  • Uttarakhand
  • Uttar Pradesh

Conditions Supporting Rabi Crops

  • Cool winter season.
  • Suitable soil conditions.
  • Availability of winter precipitation.
  • Western temperate cyclones provide rainfall during winter.
  • Green Revolution contributed to increased production.
Green Revolution Regions: Punjab, Haryana, Western Uttar Pradesh and parts of Rajasthan.

3.2 Kharif Season

Sowing: With the onset of monsoon.

Harvesting: September–October.

Major Kharif Crops

  • Paddy
  • Maize
  • Jowar
  • Bajra
  • Tur (Arhar)
  • Moong
  • Urad
  • Cotton
  • Jute
  • Groundnut
  • Soyabean

Major Rice-Growing Regions

  • Assam
  • West Bengal
  • Coastal Odisha
  • Andhra Pradesh
  • Telangana
  • Tamil Nadu
  • Kerala
  • Maharashtra
  • Uttar Pradesh
  • Bihar
  • Punjab
  • Haryana

Three Paddy Crops in Eastern States

In Assam, West Bengal and Odisha, three crops of paddy are grown in one year.

3.3 Zaid Season

Zaid is a short cropping season between Rabi and Kharif.

Season: Summer months.

Major Zaid Crops

  • Watermelon
  • Muskmelon
  • Cucumber
  • Vegetables
  • Fodder crops
Important Fact: Sugarcane takes almost one year to grow.

6. Wheat

Introduction

Wheat is the second most important cereal crop in India.

Characteristics

  • It is a Rabi crop.
  • It is the main food crop of North and North-Western India.
  • It requires a cool growing season.
  • Bright sunshine is required during ripening.
  • Requires 50–75 cm annual rainfall.
  • Rainfall should be evenly distributed over the growing season.

Two Important Wheat-Growing Zones

  1. Ganga-Satluj plains in the North-West.
  2. Black soil region of the Deccan.

Major Wheat-Producing States

17. Technological and Institutional Reforms

Introduction

Agriculture has been practised in India for thousands of years. However, continuous use of land without compatible technological and institutional changes has hindered agricultural development.

Major Problems of Indian Agriculture

  • Dependence on monsoon rainfall.
  • Dependence on natural soil fertility.
  • Fragmentation of agricultural land.
  • Small and uneconomical landholdings.
  • Lack of alternative livelihood opportunities.
  • Need for modern technology.
  • Need for institutional reforms.
  • Challenges posed by a growing population.

17.1 Institutional Reforms after Independence

The government introduced several institutional reforms to improve agricultural conditions.

Major Institutional Reforms

  • Collectivisation
  • Consolidation of landholdings
  • Cooperation
  • Abolition of Zamindari

Land Reforms

Land reform was the main focus of India's First Five Year Plan.

Objectives

  • Reduce fragmentation of land.
  • Consolidate small landholdings.
  • Improve agricultural productivity.
  • Provide better opportunities to farmers.

Problem with Implementation

Although land reform laws were enacted, their implementation was lacking or lukewarm.

17.2 Green Revolution

The Green Revolution was introduced during the 1960s and 1970s to improve agricultural production.

Main Features

  • Use of HYV seeds.
  • Increased use of fertilisers.
  • Expansion of irrigation facilities.
  • Use of modern agricultural technology.
  • Increased agricultural productivity.

Limitation

Agricultural development became concentrated in a few selected areas.

17.3 White Revolution

White Revolution is associated with Operation Flood. It was one of the strategies initiated to improve the condition of Indian agriculture and related rural livelihoods.

White Revolution = Operation Flood = Milk Production.

17.4 Comprehensive Land Development Programme

During the 1980s and 1990s, a comprehensive land development programme was initiated.

It included both:

  • Institutional reforms.
  • Technological reforms.

Important Measures

  • Crop insurance against natural calamities.
  • Establishment of Grameen banks.
  • Cooperative societies.
  • Banks providing loans at lower interest rates.
  • Kisan Credit Card.
  • Personal Accident Insurance Scheme.
  • Weather bulletins.
  • Agricultural programmes on radio and television.
  • Minimum Support Price.
  • Remunerative and procurement prices.

17.5 Crop Insurance

Crop insurance was introduced against agricultural risks.

Risks Covered

  • Drought
  • Flood
  • Cyclone
  • Fire
  • Disease

Importance

It provides protection to farmers against agricultural losses caused by natural calamities and crop-related risks.

17.6 Grameen Banks and Cooperative Societies

Main Objectives

  • Provide loans.
  • Reduce dependence on moneylenders.
  • Provide credit at lower interest rates.
  • Support agricultural development.

17.7 Kisan Credit Card (KCC)

Kisan Credit Card is an important scheme introduced by the Government of India for farmers.

It provides agricultural credit facilities to farmers.

17.8 Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS)

PAIS is another scheme mentioned in the NCERT chapter for the benefit of farmers.

17.9 Minimum Support Price (MSP)

The government announces Minimum Support Price for important crops.

Objectives

  • Protect farmers from exploitation.
  • Reduce the role of speculators and middlemen.
  • Provide remunerative prices.
  • Support agricultural income.

Three Important Price Terms

  • Minimum Support Price
  • Remunerative Price
  • Procurement Price
18. Bhoodan–Gramdan Movement

Introduction

The Bhoodan–Gramdan movement was initiated by Vinoba Bhave.

18.1 Vinoba Bhave

  • Mahatma Gandhi declared Vinoba Bhave as his spiritual heir.
  • He participated in Satyagraha.
  • He supported Gandhi's concept of Gram Swarajya.
  • After Gandhi's martyrdom, Vinoba Bhave undertook a Padyatra.
  • His objective was to spread Gandhi's message throughout India.

18.2 Beginning of Bhoodan Movement

The movement began with an incident at Pochampalli in Andhra Pradesh.

Sequence of Events

  1. Vinoba Bhave was delivering a lecture at Pochampalli.
  2. Some poor landless villagers demanded land for their economic well-being.
  3. Vinoba Bhave could not immediately promise land.
  4. He assured them that he would discuss the matter with the Government of India.
  5. He suggested cooperative farming.
  6. Shri Ram Chandra Reddy stood up.
  7. He offered 80 acres of land for distribution among 80 landless villagers.
Important Fact: 80 acres of land were donated by Shri Ram Chandra Reddy for 80 landless villagers at Pochampalli.

18.3 Gramdan

Later, Vinoba Bhave travelled throughout India and spread his ideas. Some zamindars and landowners offered entire villages for distribution among landless people. This was known as Gramdan.

18.4 Why Did Some Landowners Donate Land?

Some landowners donated portions of their land because of the fear of the Land Ceiling Act.

18.5 Another Name

The Bhoodan–Gramdan movement is also known as the Blood-less Revolution.

Bhoodan vs Gramdan

Short Answer Questions

Q1. Name one important beverage crop and specify the geographical conditions required for its growth.

Answer: Tea is an important beverage crop. It requires a warm and moist frost-free climate, deep and fertile well-drained soil rich in humus and organic matter, frequent showers and abundant skilled labour.

Q2. Name one staple crop of India and the regions where it is produced.

Answer: Rice is the staple food crop of a majority of Indians. It is grown in North and North-Eastern India, coastal areas and deltaic regions. Major producing states include West Bengal, Uttar Pradesh, Punjab, Bihar and Assam.

Long Answer Questions

Q1. Suggest the initiatives taken by the government to increase agricultural production.

Answer:

The Government of India introduced several technological and institutional reforms to increase agricultural production.

  • Land reforms were introduced after Independence.
  • Consolidation of landholdings was encouraged.
  • Green Revolution introduced HYV seeds, fertilisers and irrigation.
  • White Revolution (Operation Flood) was initiated.
  • Crop insurance was provided against natural calamities.
  • Grameen banks and cooperative societies were established.
  • Kisan Credit Card and Personal Accident Insurance Scheme were introduced.
  • Weather bulletins and agricultural programmes were broadcast on radio and television.
  • Minimum Support Price and procurement prices were announced to protect farmers.

Q2. Describe the geographical conditions required for the growth of rice.

Answer:

Rice is a Kharif crop and requires a hot and humid climate. It requires temperatures above 25°C, high humidity and annual rainfall above 100 cm. It grows well in the plains of North and North-Eastern India, coastal areas and deltaic regions. In regions with lower rainfall, canal irrigation and tube wells help in rice cultivation.
