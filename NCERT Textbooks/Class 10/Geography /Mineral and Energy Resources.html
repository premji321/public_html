1. Introduction to Minerals

Minerals are an indispensable part of human life. Almost everything used in daily life is made from minerals or mineral-based materials.

1.1 Importance of Minerals

  • Industrial and agricultural development.
  • Transportation and construction.
  • Manufacturing machinery and household goods.
  • Production of electricity and other forms of energy.
  • Economic development and infrastructure.

1.2 Everyday Examples

NCERT notes that minerals account for only about 0.3% of total nutrient intake, yet they are essential for utilising the remaining nutrients.

2. Meaning and Definition of Minerals

Definition: According to geologists, a mineral is a “homogeneous, naturally occurring substance with a definable internal structure.”

Characteristics

  1. Occurs naturally.
  2. Is homogeneous in nature.
  3. Has a definable internal structure.
  4. Possesses specific physical and chemical properties.
  5. Occurs in different forms in nature.

Physical Properties

  • Colour
  • Hardness
  • Crystal form
  • Lustre
  • Density

Diamond

Hardest mineral.

Talc

Softest mineral.

The NCERT text states that more than 2,000 minerals have been identified, but only a few are abundantly found in most rocks. Mineral formation depends on the combination of elements and the physical and chemical conditions in which the material forms.

4. Mode of Occurrence of Minerals

Minerals are usually found in “ores”. An ore is an accumulation of a mineral mixed with other elements. For commercial extraction, the mineral content must be sufficiently concentrated.

Economic viability depends on mineral concentration, ease of extraction and closeness to the market.

4.1 Minerals in Igneous and Metamorphic Rocks: Veins and Lodes

Minerals occur in cracks, crevices, faults and joints. Smaller occurrences are called veins, while larger occurrences are called lodes.

Molten or gaseous mineral materials are forced upwards through cavities, cool and solidify. Major minerals obtained this way include tin, copper, zinc and lead.

4.2 Minerals in Sedimentary Rocks

Minerals occur in beds or layers, formed by deposition, accumulation and concentration in horizontal strata. Coal and some forms of iron ore are concentrated through long periods of heat and pressure.

Gypsum, potash salt and sodium salt form through evaporation, especially in arid regions.

4.3 Residual Deposits

Decomposition of surface rocks and removal of soluble constituents leave a residual mass of weathered material containing ores. Bauxite is formed in this way.

4.4 Placer Deposits

Some minerals occur as alluvial deposits in sands of valley floors and at the base of hills. These are called placer deposits. Important examples are gold, silver, tin and platinum.

4.5 Minerals in Ocean Water and Ocean Beds

Ocean water contains vast quantities of minerals, but most are too widely diffused to be economically significant. Common salt, magnesium and bromine are derived from ocean water. Ocean beds are rich in manganese nodules.

5. Distribution of Minerals in India

Mineral resources are unevenly distributed because of differences in geological structure, geological processes and the time involved in mineral formation.

RegionMajor resources

Western and eastern flanks of peninsulaPetroleum deposits in sedimentary rocks
Gujarat and AssamPetroleum deposits
RajasthanMany non-ferrous minerals

5.1 Economic Viability

Concentration of mineral in the ore, ease of extraction and closeness to the market influence whether a deposit can be economically developed into a mine.

5.2 Rat-Hole Mining

Rat-hole mining is a traditional coal-mining method in parts of Meghalaya. Coal is extracted through long, narrow tunnels known as “rat holes”. The NCERT refers to mining in the Jowai and Cherrapunjee areas.

Meghalaya has deposits of coal, iron ore, limestone and dolomite. The textbook states that the National Green Tribunal declared such rat-hole mining activities illegal and recommended that they be stopped.

6. Classification of Minerals

Minerals are broadly classified into three categories:

  1. Metallic minerals
  2. Non-metallic minerals
  3. Energy minerals

6.1 Metallic Minerals

Metallic minerals contain metals and are important for manufacturing and industrial activities.

  • Ferrous: iron ore and manganese.
  • Non-ferrous: copper, bauxite, lead, zinc and gold.

6.2 Non-Metallic Minerals

Examples include mica and limestone.

6.3 Energy Minerals

Examples include coal, petroleum and natural gas. Uranium is used for generating nuclear energy.

7. Ferrous Minerals

Ferrous minerals account for about three-fourths of the total value of production of metallic minerals, according to NCERT. They provide a strong base for metallurgical industries.

7.1 Iron Ore

Iron ore is the basic mineral and the backbone of industrial development. India has fairly abundant resources of good-quality iron ore.

Magnetite

  • Finest quality of iron ore.
  • Iron content up to 70%.
  • Excellent magnetic qualities.
  • Valuable in the electrical industry.

Hematite

  • Most important industrial iron ore in terms of quantity used.
  • Iron content approximately 50–60%.
  • Iron content is slightly lower than magnetite.
According to the NCERT's 2018–19 data, Odisha, Chhattisgarh, Karnataka and Jharkhand together accounted for about 97% of India's iron ore production.

7.2 Four Major Iron Ore Belts

A. Odisha–Jharkhand Belt

  • Odisha: high-grade hematite in Badampahar mines, Mayurbhanj and Kendujhar districts.
  • Jharkhand: hematite in Singhbhum district; Gua and Noamundi mines.

B. Durg–Bastar–Chandrapur Belt

  • Located in Chhattisgarh and Maharashtra.
  • Very high-grade hematite in Bailadila hills, Bastar district of Chhattisgarh.
  • Bailadila range has 14 deposits of super high-grade hematite.
  • Ore is exported to Japan and South Korea through Visakhapatnam port.

C. Ballari–Chitradurga–Chikkamagaluru–Tumakuru Belt

  • Located in Karnataka.
  • Kudremukh mines are in the Western Ghats.
  • NCERT describes Kudremukh as a 100% export unit.
  • Ore is transported as slurry through a pipeline to a port near Mangaluru.

D. Maharashtra–Goa Belt

  • Includes Goa and Ratnagiri district of Maharashtra.
  • Ores are not of very high quality but are efficiently exploited.
  • Iron ore is exported through Marmagao port.

7.3 Manganese

Manganese is mainly used in manufacturing steel and ferro-manganese alloy. Nearly 10 kg of manganese is required to manufacture one tonne of steel. It is also used in bleaching powder, insecticides and paints.

8. Non-Ferrous Minerals: Copper and Bauxite

India's reserves and production of non-ferrous minerals are not very satisfactory. Copper, bauxite, lead, zinc and gold are important for metallurgical, engineering and electrical industries.

8.1 Copper

  • India is critically deficient in copper reserves and production.
  • Copper is malleable and ductile.
  • It is a good conductor.
  • Used in electrical cables, electronics and chemical industries.

8.2 Bauxite

Bauxite is a clay-like substance from which alumina and subsequently aluminium are obtained. Deposits form through decomposition of rocks rich in aluminium silicates.

Importance of Aluminium

  • Combines strength with extreme lightness.
  • Good conductivity.
  • Great malleability.

Important bauxite regions

  • Maikal Hills.
  • Bilaspur–Katni plateau region.
  • Panchpatmali deposits in Koraput district, Odisha.

9. Non-Metallic Minerals: Mica and Limestone

9.1 Mica

Mica is made up of a series of plates or leaves and splits easily into thin sheets. It may be clear, black, green, red, yellow or brown.

Properties

  • Excellent dielectric strength.
  • Low power-loss factor.
  • Good insulating properties.
  • Resistance to high voltage.

Mica is indispensable in electrical and electronic industries.

Major mica-producing regions

  • Jharkhand: northern edge of Chota Nagpur Plateau; Koderma–Gaya–Hazaribagh belt.
  • Rajasthan: around Ajmer.
  • Andhra Pradesh: Nellore mica belt.

9.2 Limestone

Limestone is associated with rocks composed of calcium carbonates or calcium and magnesium carbonates. It occurs in sedimentary rocks of most geological formations.

Uses: Basic raw material for the cement industry and essential for smelting iron ore in a blast furnace.

10. Hazards of Mining

Health hazards for miners

  • Dust and noxious fumes can cause pulmonary diseases.
  • Mine roofs may collapse.
  • Flooding or inundation may occur.
  • Fires in coal mines pose a constant threat.

Environmental hazards

  • Contamination of water sources.
  • Land and soil degradation.
  • Stream and river pollution due to waste and slurry.
  • Air pollution caused by dust from mining areas.

Preventive measures

  • Stricter safety regulations.
  • Effective implementation of environmental laws.
  • Safe disposal of mining waste.
  • Monitoring of air and water quality.

11. Conservation of Minerals

Minerals are essential for industry and agriculture, but workable mineral deposits form only an insignificant fraction of the Earth's crust. Mineral formation takes millions of years, and replenishment is extremely slow compared with current consumption.

Why conserve minerals?

  1. Mineral resources are finite and non-renewable.
  2. Geological processes form minerals very slowly.
  3. Continued extraction increases costs.
  4. Mining shifts to greater depths as accessible deposits are exhausted.
  5. Ore quality may decrease with continued extraction.

Methods of conservation

  • Use mineral resources in a planned and sustainable manner.
  • Develop improved technologies to use low-grade ores at lower costs.
  • Recycle metals.
  • Reuse scrap metals.
  • Use substitutes wherever suitable.
  • Reduce wastage and unnecessary consumption.
Remember: Mineral resources are finite and non-renewable. Recycling, scrap reuse and substitutes help conserve them.

12. Energy Resources: Meaning and Classification

Energy is needed for cooking, lighting, heating, transportation and operating industrial machinery.

Classification

Conventional sources

Firewood, cattle dung cakes, coal, petroleum, natural gas and electricity (hydel and thermal).

Non-conventional sources

Solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas and atomic energy.

Firewood and cattle dung cakes are common in rural India. NCERT notes that their continued use is difficult due to decreasing forest area, and dung cakes are discouraged because dung is valuable manure for agriculture.

13. Conventional Energy: Coal

Coal is the most abundantly available fossil fuel in India, as described in the NCERT chapter. It provides a substantial part of the country's energy needs and is used for power generation, industry and domestic requirements.

13.1 Formation of Coal

Coal forms through compression of plant material over millions of years. Its variety depends on the degree of compression, depth of burial and temperature.

13.2 Types of Coal

TypeCharacteristics
PeatDecaying plants in swamps; low carbon, high moisture and low heating capacity.
LigniteLow-grade brown coal; soft and high in moisture. Neyveli in Tamil Nadu has principal lignite reserves.
BituminousFormed at greater depth and higher temperature; most popular coal in commercial use.
AnthraciteHighest-quality hard coal.

Metallurgical coal is high-grade bituminous coal with special value for smelting iron in blast furnaces.

13.3 Geological Ages and Distribution

Gondwana coal

  • A little over 200 million years old.
  • Major resources in Damodar Valley.
  • Jharia, Raniganj and Bokaro are important coalfields.
  • Other deposits: Godavari, Mahanadi, Son and Wardha valleys.

Tertiary coal

  • About 55 million years old.
  • Found in Meghalaya, Assam, Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland.

13.4 Why are industries located near coalfields?

Coal is bulky and loses weight when used because it is reduced to ash. Heavy industries and thermal power stations are therefore often located on or near coalfields to reduce transportation costs.

14. Petroleum

Petroleum, also called mineral oil, is the next major energy source in India after coal.

Uses

  • Fuel for heat and lighting.
  • Lubricants for machinery.
  • Raw material for manufacturing industries.

Petroleum refineries act as a nodal industry for synthetic textile, fertiliser and numerous chemical industries.

Occurrence: Anticlines and Fault Traps

Most petroleum occurrences in India are associated with anticlines and fault traps in Tertiary rock formations. In anticlines or domes, oil is trapped in the crest of an upfold. The oil-bearing layer is porous limestone or sandstone, while non-porous layers prevent oil from moving. Natural gas, being lighter, usually occurs above oil.

Major petroleum-producing regions

  • Mumbai High: important offshore field.
  • Gujarat: Ankleshwar is an important field.
  • Assam: oldest oil-producing state; Digboi, Naharkatiya and Moran-Hugrijan are important oilfields.
−

15. Natural Gas

Natural gas is found with petroleum deposits and is released when crude oil is brought to the surface.

Uses

  • Domestic and industrial fuel.
  • Electricity generation.
  • Heating in industries.
  • Raw material for chemical, petrochemical and fertiliser industries.
  • Transport fuel and cooking fuel.

CNG and PNG

  • CNG: Compressed Natural Gas, used as a transport fuel.
  • PNG: Piped Natural Gas, used as a cooking fuel in homes.

Major reserves

  • Mumbai High and allied fields along the west coast.
  • Cambay Basin.
  • Krishna-Godavari Basin along the east coast.

Hazira–Vijaipur–Jagdishpur (HVJ) Pipeline

  • Constructed by GAIL (India).
  • About 1,700 km long, as stated in NCERT.
  • Linked Mumbai High and Bassein gas fields with fertiliser, power and industrial complexes in western and northern India.
  • Provided impetus to the Indian gas market.

16. Electricity: Hydel and Thermal Power

Electricity has a wide range of applications. Per capita electricity consumption is considered an index of development.

16.1 Hydroelectricity

Fast-flowing water drives hydro turbines to generate electricity. Hydroelectricity is a renewable resource.

NCERT examples of multipurpose projects include Bhakra Nangal, Damodar Valley Corporation and the Kopili Hydel Project.

16.2 Thermal Electricity

Thermal electricity is generated using coal, petroleum and natural gas. These fuels are burnt to produce heat, which is used to drive turbines and generate electricity. Thermal power stations use non-renewable fossil fuels.

Uses flowing water.Uses coal, petroleum or natural gas.

17. Non-Conventional Sources of Energy

Growing energy consumption increases dependence on fossil fuels. Rising prices, potential shortages and environmental problems make renewable energy development important.

17.1 Nuclear or Atomic Energy

Obtained by altering the structure of atoms. Energy released as heat is used to generate electric power. Uranium and thorium are used for nuclear power. NCERT mentions uranium and thorium in Jharkhand and the Aravalli ranges of Rajasthan; monazite sands of Kerala are rich in thorium.

17.2 Solar Energy

India is a tropical country with enormous possibilities for solar energy. Photovoltaic technology converts sunlight directly into electricity.

  • Useful in rural and remote areas.
  • Reduces dependence on firewood and dung cakes.
  • Supports environmental conservation.
  • Helps preserve cattle dung as manure for agriculture.

17.3 Wind Energy

India has great potential for wind power. NCERT identifies the largest wind farm cluster from Nagarcoil to Madurai in Tamil Nadu.

Other important regions: Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, Gujarat, Kerala, Maharashtra and Lakshadweep. Nagarcoil and Jaisalmer are noted for effective use of wind energy.

17.4 Biogas

Biogas is produced by decomposition of shrubs, farm waste, animal waste and human waste. Plants using cattle dung are called Gobar Gas Plants.

  • Provides domestic fuel.
  • Has higher thermal efficiency than kerosene, dung cakes and charcoal, as stated in NCERT.
  • Improves manure quality.
  • Reduces burning of fuelwood and cow-dung cakes.
  • Helps prevent loss of trees and manure.

17.5 Tidal Energy

Ocean tides can generate electricity. Floodgate dams are built across inlets. During high tide water enters and is trapped; when the tide falls, the retained water flows back through a pipe and drives a turbine.

Important Indian regions: Gulf of Khambhat, Gulf of Kuchchh (Gujarat) and the Gangetic delta in the Sundarban region of West Bengal.

17.6 Geothermal Energy

Geothermal energy is heat and electricity produced using heat from the Earth's interior. Underground water absorbs heat from rocks and may rise as steam, which drives turbines.

NCERT mentions two experimental projects: Parvati Valley near Manikaran in Himachal Pradesh and Puga Valley in Ladakh.

18. Conservation of Energy Resources

Energy is a basic requirement for economic development. Agriculture, industry, transport, commercial and domestic sectors all need energy. Consumption has steadily risen.

Why conserve energy?

  • Energy demand is increasing.
  • Fossil fuels are limited and non-renewable.
  • Heavy dependence on fossil fuels creates environmental problems.
  • Energy security is important for future development.

Two pillars of sustainable energy

  1. Promotion of energy conservation.
  2. Increased use of renewable energy sources.

Practical steps

  • Use public transport instead of individual vehicles.
  • Switch off electricity when not in use.
  • Use power-saving devices.
  • Adopt non-conventional sources of energy.
  • Avoid unnecessary fuel and electricity consumption.
NCERT statement: “Energy saved is energy produced.”

23. NCERT Short Answer Questions

Q1. Distinguish between ferrous and non-ferrous minerals.

Ferrous minerals contain iron, such as iron ore and manganese. Non-ferrous minerals do not contain iron as their principal metallic component, such as copper, bauxite, lead and zinc.

Q2. Distinguish between conventional and non-conventional sources of energy.

Conventional sources include firewood, coal, petroleum, natural gas and electricity. Non-conventional sources include solar, wind, tidal, geothermal, biogas and atomic energy.

Q3. What is a mineral?

A mineral is a homogeneous, naturally occurring substance with a definable internal structure. Minerals have specific physical and chemical properties.

Q4. How are minerals formed in igneous and metamorphic rocks?

Minerals occur in cracks, crevices, faults and joints. Molten and gaseous materials move upwards through cavities, cool and solidify, forming deposits known as veins and lodes.

Q5. Why do we need to conserve mineral resources?

Minerals are finite and non-renewable. Their formation takes millions of years, while consumption is rapid. Recycling, reuse, improved technology and planned utilisation are therefore necessary.

24. NCERT Long Answer Questions

Q1. Describe the distribution of coal in India.

Coal occurs in two major geological formations: Gondwana and Tertiary. Gondwana coal is a little over 200 million years old. Major resources are located in the Damodar Valley, where Jharia, Raniganj and Bokaro are important coalfields. Other deposits occur in the Godavari, Mahanadi, Son and Wardha valleys. Tertiary coal is about 55 million years old and occurs mainly in Meghalaya, Assam, Arunachal Pradesh and Nagaland. Coal is important for electricity generation, industry and domestic energy requirements.

Q2. Why does solar energy have a bright future in India?

India is a tropical country and receives abundant sunlight. Photovoltaic technology converts sunlight directly into electricity. Solar energy is renewable and useful in rural and remote areas. It can reduce dependence on firewood and cattle dung cakes, help conserve forests and support environmental protection. It can also provide electricity in areas where conventional infrastructure is limited.
