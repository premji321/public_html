    Chapter Introduction

    Print is present in almost every aspect of modern life. Books, journals, newspapers, advertisements, calendars, diaries, theatre programmes, official circulars, posters and printed images are all examples of print culture.

    This chapter explains how printing developed in East Asia, spread to Europe and reached India. It also examines how printing transformed education, religion, society, politics, women's lives, working-class communities and the growth of nationalism.

    Central Theme: Print was not merely a new technology for producing books. It transformed the circulation of knowledge, encouraged debate, challenged established authority and helped shape the modern world.
    1. THE FIRST PRINTED BOOKS – ORIGIN IN EAST ASIA

    1.1 Development of Printing in China

    • The earliest print technology developed in China, Japan and Korea.
    • Initially, printing was done by hand using wooden blocks.
    • From AD 594 onwards, Chinese books were printed by rubbing paper against an inked wooden block.
    • Paper was also invented in China.
    • Because the paper was thin and porous, both sides could not be printed easily.
    • Chinese books were therefore folded and stitched at the side in an accordion format.
    • Skilled craftsmen reproduced calligraphy with remarkable accuracy.

    1.2 Role of the Imperial State in China

    • The imperial state was the major producer of printed material for a long period.
    • China had a large bureaucratic system.
    • Officials were recruited through civil service examinations.
    • Textbooks for these examinations were printed in large numbers under state sponsorship.
    • From the sixteenth century, the number of examination candidates increased, which increased the volume of printed material.

    1.3 Expansion of Reading Culture in China

    • By the seventeenth century, urban culture had developed.
    • Printing was no longer restricted to scholar-officials.
    • Merchants used printed material to collect trade information.
    • Reading increasingly became a leisure activity.
    • Readers preferred fictional narratives, poetry, autobiographies, anthologies of literary masterpieces and romantic plays.
    • Rich women began reading and publishing poetry and plays.
    • Wives of scholar-officials published their works, while courtesans wrote about their lives.
    • Western printing techniques and mechanical presses were imported in the late nineteenth century.
    • Shanghai became a major centre of new print culture, especially for Western-style schools.

    1.4 Print in Japan

    • Buddhist missionaries from China introduced hand-printing technology into Japan around AD 768–770.
    • The oldest Japanese printed book mentioned in this chapter is the Buddhist Diamond Sutra, printed in AD 868.
    • It contained six sheets of text and woodcut illustrations.
    • Pictures were also printed on textiles, playing cards and paper money.
    • In medieval Japan, poets and prose writers were regularly published.
    • Books became cheap and abundant.

    1.5 Edo – The Development of Urban Print Culture

    In the late eighteenth century, Edo, later known as Tokyo, developed a flourishing urban culture.

    Illustrated collections depicted artists, courtesans and teahouse gatherings.

    Libraries and bookstores contained books on:

    • Women and social life.
    • Musical instruments and calculations.
    • Tea ceremonies and flower arrangements.
    • Proper etiquette and cooking.
    • Famous places.

    1.6 Kitagawa Utamaro and Ukiyo Prints

    • Kitagawa Utamaro was born in Edo in 1753.
    • He became famous for an art form called ukiyo.
    • Ukiyo means pictures of the floating world.
    • It depicted ordinary human experiences, particularly urban experiences.
    • His prints travelled to the United States and Europe.
    • They influenced artists such as Manet, Monet and Van Gogh.
    • Publishers such as Tsutaya Juzaburo selected subjects and commissioned artists.
    • Woodblock carvers reproduced the artist's lines on wooden printing blocks.
    • The original drawing was destroyed during the carving process, and only prints survived.

    JikjiKorean book printed using movable metal type in the late fourteenth century.

    2. PRINT COMES TO EUROPE

    2.1 Arrival of Paper and Printing Knowledge

    • Silk and spices from China reached Europe through the Silk Route.
    • In the eleventh century, Chinese paper reached Europe through the same route.
    • Paper made the production of handwritten manuscripts possible.
    • Manuscripts were carefully written by scribes.
    • In 1295, Marco Polo returned to Italy after spending many years exploring China.
    • He brought knowledge of Chinese woodblock printing to Europe.
    • Italians began producing books using woodblocks.
    • The technology gradually spread to other European regions.

    2.2 Handwritten and Printed Books

    Handwritten Luxury Books

    • Luxury editions were written by hand on expensive vellum.
    • They were intended for aristocratic circles and wealthy monastic libraries.
    • Some wealthy people considered printed books cheap and vulgar.

    Cheaper Printed Books

    • Merchants and university students purchased cheaper printed copies.
    • Booksellers began exporting books to different countries.
    • Book fairs were organised in different places.
    • More than 50 scribes sometimes worked for one bookseller.

    2.3 Limitations of Manuscripts

    1. Copying was expensive.
    2. Production was laborious and time-consuming.
    3. Manuscripts were fragile.
    4. They were difficult to handle and carry.
    5. Their circulation remained limited.
    6. They could not satisfy the growing demand for books.

    2.4 Growth of Woodblock Printing

    • By the early fifteenth century, woodblocks were widely used in Europe.
    • They were used for printing textiles, playing cards and religious pictures.
    • There was a growing demand for faster and cheaper reproduction of texts.
    • A new printing technology became necessary.

    2.5 Johann Gutenberg and the Printing Press

    • Johann Gutenberg was the son of a merchant.
    • He grew up on a large agricultural estate.
    • He had seen wine and olive presses during childhood.
    • He learnt stone polishing and became a master goldsmith.
    • He acquired expertise in making lead moulds for trinkets.
    • He adapted existing technology to develop his printing press.
    • The olive press provided the model for the printing press.
    • Moulds were used to cast metal types for letters.
    • By 1448, Gutenberg had perfected the printing system.
    • His first printed book was the Bible.
    • About 180 copies were printed in approximately three years.

    2.6 Features of the Gutenberg Press

    • It used movable metal types.
    • Metal types were created for each of the 26 Roman alphabet characters.
    • The types could be rearranged to compose different words.
    • A screw and handle operated the press.
    • The platen pressed paper against the printing type.
    • It could print about 250 sheets on one side per hour.
    • It remained the basic printing technology for approximately 300 years.

    2.7 Appearance of Early Printed Books

    • Printed books initially resembled handwritten manuscripts.
    • Metal letters imitated ornamental handwriting.
    • Borders were decorated by hand.
    • Illustrations were painted manually.
    • Space was sometimes left for purchasers to choose their own decoration.
    • Different copies of Gutenberg's Bible were not exactly identical.
    • Colour was added by hand after printing.

    2.8 Spread of Printing in Europe

    • Between 1450 and 1550, printing presses were established in most European countries.
    • German printers travelled to other countries to establish new presses.
    • During the second half of the fifteenth century, around 20 million printed books entered European markets.
    • In the sixteenth century, the number increased to approximately 200 million.
    Print Revolution: The shift from hand printing to mechanical printing is known as the Print Revolution.

    2.9 Important Printing Terms

    TermMeaning
    VellumParchment made from animal skin.
    PlatenA board pressed onto paper to create an impression from the type.
    CompositorA person who composes text for printing.
    GalleyA metal frame in which types are laid and text is composed.
    Movable TypeReusable individual letters arranged to create different texts.
    3. THE PRINT REVOLUTION AND ITS IMPACT

    3.1 Meaning of the Print Revolution

    The Print Revolution was not merely a new method of producing books. It transformed people's lives and their relationship with information, knowledge, institutions and authorities.

    3.2 A New Reading Public

    Before Printing

    • Reading was mainly restricted to elites.
    • Books were expensive and difficult to produce.
    • Common people depended on oral culture.
    • Sacred texts were read aloud.
    • Ballads were recited and folk tales were narrated.
    • Knowledge was transmitted orally.

    After Printing

    • Printing reduced the cost of books.
    • Production required less time and labour.
    • Multiple copies could be produced more easily.
    • Books reached a wider readership.
    • A new reading public emerged.
    Hearing Public: People who mainly received knowledge by listening.
    Reading Public: People who accessed information through reading printed material.

    3.3 Relationship Between Oral and Print Culture

    • Literacy remained low in many European countries until the twentieth century.
    • Publishers wanted to reach people who could not read.
    • Popular ballads and folk tales were printed.
    • Books contained illustrations.
    • Printed stories were sung and recited at village gatherings and in town taverns.
    • Oral culture entered print, while printed material continued to be transmitted orally.
    • The distinction between hearing and reading cultures became blurred.

    3.4 Religious Debates and Fear of Print

    • Print allowed different opinions to circulate widely.
    • People could challenge established authorities.
    • Printed messages could persuade people to think differently and take action.
    • Some religious authorities and monarchs feared uncontrolled circulation of ideas.
    • They worried that rebellious and irreligious thoughts might spread.
    • They feared that the authority of established literature would be weakened.

    3.5 Martin Luther and the Protestant Reformation

    • In 1517, Martin Luther wrote the Ninety Five Theses.
    • He criticised many practices and rituals of the Roman Catholic Church.
    • A printed copy was posted on a church door in Wittenberg.
    • It challenged the Church to debate his ideas.
    • His writings were reproduced in large numbers and read widely.
    • This contributed to division within the Church and the beginning of the Protestant Reformation.
    • His translation of the New Testament sold 5,000 copies within a few weeks.
    • A second edition appeared within three months.
    Protestant Reformation: A sixteenth-century movement to reform the Roman Catholic Church. Martin Luther was one of its main reformers.

    3.6 Print and Dissent – Menocchio

    • Menocchio was a miller in Italy in the sixteenth century.
    • He read books available in his locality.
    • He developed his own interpretation of the Bible.
    • His views about God and Creation angered the Roman Catholic Church.
    • He was tried twice by the Inquisition and ultimately executed.
    • The Church imposed severe controls over publishers and booksellers.
    • It began maintaining an Index of Prohibited Books from 1558.

    3.7 Erasmus and the Fear of Books

    • Erasmus was a Latin scholar and Catholic reformer.
    • He criticised excesses within Catholicism but kept his distance from Luther.
    • In Adages (1508), he expressed anxiety about the large number of printed books.
    • He feared that worthless, ignorant, scandalous, irreligious and seditious books could harm scholarship.
    • He believed that excessive publication could reduce the value of useful books.

    3.8 Important Terms

    TermMeaning
    InquisitionFormer Roman Catholic court for identifying and punishing heretics.
    HereticalBeliefs that do not follow accepted Church teachings.
    SatietyA state of being fulfilled beyond satisfaction.
    SeditiousAction, speech or writing seen as opposing the government.
    4. THE READING MANIA IN EUROPE

    4.1 Growth of Literacy

    • During the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, literacy increased in most parts of Europe.
    • Churches of different denominations established village schools.
    • Education reached peasants and artisans.
    • By the end of the eighteenth century, literacy in some parts of Europe reached 60–80 per cent.
    • There was a widespread reading mania.
    • Printers produced books in increasing numbers.

    4.2 New Forms of Popular Literature

    PublicationDetails
    AlmanacsAnnual publications containing astronomical data, calendar information, movements of the sun and moon, tides and eclipses.
    BalladsHistorical accounts or folk tales in verse.
    ChapbooksPocket-sized books sold by travelling pedlars called chapmen.
    Penny ChapbooksCheap books sold for one penny in England.
    Bibliothèque BleueLow-priced French books printed on poor-quality paper and bound in cheap blue covers.
    RomancesShort entertaining stories, sometimes printed on four to six pages.
    HistoriesStories about the past.

    4.3 Booksellers and Pedlars

    • Booksellers employed pedlars to travel through villages.
    • They carried small books for sale.
    • Cheap publications made reading accessible to ordinary people.
    • In England, chapmen sold penny chapbooks.

    4.4 Growth of Newspapers and Periodicals

    • The periodical press developed from the early eighteenth century.
    • It combined current affairs with entertainment.
    • Newspapers and journals carried information about wars and trade.
    • They also reported developments in other places.

    4.5 Spread of Scientific and Philosophical Ideas

    • Scientific and philosophical ideas became more accessible.
    • Ancient and medieval scientific texts were compiled and published.
    • Maps and scientific diagrams were widely printed.
    • Isaac Newton's discoveries reached a wider scientific readership.
    • Writings of Thomas Paine, Voltaire and Jean-Jacques Rousseau were widely printed.
    • Ideas about science, reason and rationality entered popular literature.

    4.6 James Lackington's Observation

    In 1791, London publisher James Lackington recorded that book sales had increased greatly. Poor farmers and country people who had earlier spent winter evenings telling stories were now listening to their children read tales and romances.

    Important names: Isaac Newton, Thomas Paine, Voltaire, Jean-Jacques Rousseau and James Lackington.
    5. PRINT CULTURE, ENLIGHTENMENT AND THE FRENCH REVOLUTION

    5.1 “Tremble, Therefore, Tyrants of the World!”

    • By the mid-eighteenth century, many people believed books spread progress and enlightenment.
    • Books were seen as instruments to change society.
    • People believed books could challenge despotism and tyranny.
    • Reason and intellect were expected to replace superstition and arbitrary authority.

    5.2 Louise-Sébastien Mercier

    • He was a French novelist of the eighteenth century.
    • He believed the printing press was a powerful instrument of progress.
    • He considered public opinion a force capable of challenging despotism.
    • In his novels, heroes were transformed through reading.
    • He believed books could enlighten readers and challenge tyranny.

    5.3 Print Culture and the French Revolution

    Historians have presented three major arguments explaining how print culture helped create conditions for the French Revolution.

    First: Spread of Enlightenment Ideas

    • Print popularised the ideas of Enlightenment thinkers.
    • Writers criticised tradition, superstition and despotism.
    • They supported reason rather than custom.
    • They questioned the sacred authority of the Church.
    • They criticised the despotic power of the state.
    • Voltaire and Rousseau were widely read.

    Second: Culture of Dialogue and Debate

    • Print created a new public culture of debate.
    • Values, norms and institutions were re-evaluated.
    • People recognised the importance of questioning existing beliefs.
    • New ideas of social revolution emerged.

    Third: Criticism of the Monarchy

    • By the 1780s, literature mocking royalty became widespread.
    • Publications criticised the morality of the royal family.
    • Cartoons showed the monarchy enjoying luxury while ordinary people suffered.
    • Such literature sometimes circulated underground.
    • It contributed to hostile sentiments against the monarchy.

    5.4 Did Print Directly Cause the French Revolution?

    • People did not read only revolutionary literature.
    • They also encountered monarchical and Church propaganda.
    • They did not accept everything they read.
    • They interpreted ideas in their own ways.
    • Print did not directly control people's minds.
    • It opened up the possibility of thinking differently.
    Important conclusion: Print culture helped create an atmosphere of questioning and debate, but it did not automatically or single-handedly cause the French Revolution.

    5.5 Cartoon of the French Revolution

    The NCERT cartoon on the nobility and common people before the French Revolution depicts peasants, artisans and workers suffering while the nobility enjoyed life. Such images criticised social inequality and helped shape public opinion.

    6. THE NINETEENTH CENTURY – CHILDREN, WOMEN AND WORKERS

    6.1 Expansion of Mass Literacy

    • The nineteenth century saw major growth in literacy in Europe.
    • Children, women and workers became important new categories of readers.
    • Primary education became compulsory from the late nineteenth century.

    6.2 Children and Print

    • Children became an important reading group.
    • School textbooks became essential to publishers.
    • A children's press was established in France in 1857.
    • It published new works, fairy tales and folk tales.

    6.3 Grimm Brothers

    • The Grimm Brothers in Germany collected traditional folk tales from peasants.
    • The collection was published in 1812.
    • Stories were edited before publication.
    • Material considered unsuitable for children or vulgar to elites was removed.
    • Print preserved old tales but also changed their form.

    6.4 Women as Readers and Writers

    • Women became important readers as well as writers.
    • Penny magazines were especially intended for women.
    • Manuals taught proper behaviour and housekeeping.
    • Women became important readers of novels.

    6.5 Important Women Novelists

    • Jane Austen.
    • The Brontë sisters.
    • George Eliot.

    Their writings presented women as individuals with will, strength of personality, determination and the power to think.

    6.6 Workers and Lending Libraries

    • Lending libraries existed from the seventeenth century onwards.
    • In nineteenth-century England, they educated white-collar workers, artisans and lower-middle-class people.
    • Working-class people sometimes educated themselves and wrote their own works.
    • As working hours gradually shortened from the mid-nineteenth century, workers gained time for self-improvement.
    • They wrote political tracts and autobiographies.

    6.7 Thomas Wood and Maxim Gorky

    • Thomas Wood was a Yorkshire mechanic.
    • He rented old newspapers and read them by firelight because he could not afford candles.
    • Maxim Gorky's My Childhood and My University provide glimpses of poor people's struggles to gain education and reading opportunities.

    6.8 Penny Magazine

    • Published in England between 1832 and 1835.
    • Published by the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge.
    • Aimed primarily at the working class.

    6.9 Further Innovations in Printing Technology

    PeriodDevelopment
    Late eighteenth centuryPrinting presses began to be made of metal.
    Mid-nineteenth centuryRichard M. Hoe perfected the power-driven cylindrical press.
    Late nineteenth centuryOffset press was developed.
    Early twentieth centuryElectrically operated presses accelerated printing.

    6.10 Richard M. Hoe's Cylindrical Press

    • Perfected by Richard M. Hoe of New York.
    • Could print 8,000 sheets per hour.
    • Particularly useful for newspapers.

    6.11 Offset Press and Other Improvements

    • The offset press could print up to six colours at a time.
    • Paper-feeding methods improved.
    • Printing plates became better.
    • Automatic paper reels were introduced.
    • Photoelectric controls of colour registration were developed.
    • These improvements transformed the appearance of printed texts.

    6.12 Marketing Strategies of Publishers

    • Nineteenth-century periodicals published important novels in serial form.
    • In the 1920s, cheap popular works were sold in England as the Shilling Series.
    • Dust covers and book jackets became twentieth-century innovations.
    • During the Great Depression of the 1930s, publishers introduced cheap paperback editions to sustain sales.
    • Printed advertisements appeared on street walls, railway platforms and public buildings.

    6.13 Advertisement and Public Opinion

    Advertisements helped publishers and businesses attract readers and buyers. Printed notices and advertisements became a visible part of public spaces. People could respond differently to the same printed material.

    7. INDIA AND THE WORLD OF PRINT

    7.1 Manuscripts Before the Age of Print

    • India had a rich tradition of handwritten manuscripts.
    • Manuscripts were written in Sanskrit, Arabic, Persian and various vernacular languages.
    • They were copied on palm leaves or handmade paper.
    • Pages were sometimes beautifully illustrated.
    • They were preserved between wooden covers or sewn together.
    • Manuscripts continued to be produced until the late nineteenth century, even after printing was introduced.

    7.2 Limitations of Indian Manuscripts

    • They were expensive and fragile.
    • They required careful handling.
    • Different writing styles made reading difficult.
    • They were not widely used in everyday life.

    7.3 Pre-colonial Bengal and Learning

    • Pre-colonial Bengal had an extensive network of village primary schools.
    • Students often learnt to write without reading texts.
    • Teachers dictated passages from memory.
    • Students wrote down the dictated passages.
    • Some became literate without actually reading books.

    7.4 Introduction of the Printing Press in India

    • The printing press first came to Goa with Portuguese missionaries in the mid-sixteenth century.
    • Jesuit priests learnt Konkani.
    • They printed several religious tracts.
    • By 1674, about 50 books had been printed in Konkani and Kanara languages.
    • Catholic priests printed the first Tamil book in 1579 at Cochin.
    • The first Malayalam book was printed in 1713.
    • By 1710, Dutch Protestant missionaries had printed 32 Tamil texts.
    • Many of these were translations of older works.

    7.5 Beginning of English Printing in India

    • The English-language press developed relatively late in India.
    • The East India Company began importing presses from the late seventeenth century.
    • In 1768, William Bolts placed a public notice in Calcutta about the need for a printing press.
    • Bolts left for England soon afterwards, and the proposal did not materialise.

    7.6 James Augustus Hickey and Bengal Gazette

    • In 1780, James Augustus Hickey began editing the Bengal Gazette.
    • It was a weekly magazine.
    • It described itself as a commercial paper open to all but influenced by none.
    • Hickey published advertisements, including advertisements concerning the import and sale of slaves.
    • He also published gossip about senior Company officials.
    • Governor-General Warren Hastings was angered by Hickey's publications.
    • Hastings persecuted Hickey and encouraged officially sanctioned newspapers.

    7.7 Indian-Owned Newspapers

    • By the end of the eighteenth century, several newspapers and journals appeared.
    • Indians also began publishing newspapers.
    • The first Indian weekly newspaper mentioned in the chapter was the Bengal Gazette published by Gangadhar Bhattacharya.
    • Gangadhar Bhattacharya was close to Rammohun Roy.
    Important distinction: James Augustus Hickey began editing the Bengal Gazette in 1780. Gangadhar Bhattacharya later brought out an Indian weekly Bengal Gazette.
    8. RELIGIOUS REFORM AND PUBLIC DEBATES IN INDIA

    8.1 Development of Public Debates

    • From the early nineteenth century, religious issues became subjects of intense debate.
    • Different groups interpreted religious beliefs in different ways.
    • Some groups criticised existing practices and demanded reform.
    • Other groups defended orthodox traditions.
    • Printed tracts and newspapers spread these arguments.
    • A wider public could participate in discussions.

    8.2 Religious and Social Issues in Bengal

    • Widow immolation.
    • Monotheism.
    • Brahmanical priesthood.
    • Idolatry.

    To reach wider audiences, ideas were published in the everyday spoken language of ordinary people.

    8.3 Important Newspapers of Bengal and India

    NewspaperYearDetails
    Sambad Kaumudi1821Published by Rammohun Roy.
    Samachar Chandrika—Commissioned by Hindu orthodoxy to oppose Rammohun Roy's opinions.
    Jam-i-Jahan Nama1822Persian newspaper.

    Bombay Samachar1822Gujarati newspaper.

    8.4 Muslim Religious Debates and Print

    • Muslim ulama were concerned about the decline of Muslim dynasties.
    • They feared colonial rulers might encourage conversion.
    • They were concerned about possible changes to Muslim personal laws.
    • Cheap lithographic presses were used to publish religious literature.
    • Persian and Urdu translations of holy scriptures were printed.
    • Religious newspapers and tracts were circulated.

    8.5 Deoband Seminary

    • Founded in 1867.
    • Published thousands of fatwas.
    • Explained Islamic doctrines.
    • Guided Muslim readers on everyday religious conduct.
    • Different Muslim sects and seminaries published their own interpretations of faith.
    • Urdu print helped them conduct public religious debates.

    8.6 Print and Hindu Religious Literature

    • Print encouraged the reading of religious texts in vernacular languages.
    • The first printed edition of Tulsidas's Ramcharitmanas appeared in Calcutta in 1810.
    • Cheap lithographic editions spread through north Indian markets by the mid-nineteenth century.

    8.7 Important Religious Publishing Presses

    • Naval Kishore Press: Lucknow.
    • Shri Venkateshwar Press: Bombay.
    • From the 1880s, both published numerous religious texts in vernacular languages.

    8.8 Impact of Printed Religious Texts

    • Religious texts reached a wider population.
    • People could read them at different places and times.
    • Printed books could be read aloud to illiterate people.
    • Religious discussions and controversies increased.
    • Newspapers connected people from different regions.
    • Print helped create pan-Indian identities.

    8.9 Why Newspapers?

    Newspapers provided information on local interests, science, history, geography, antiquities, statistics, trade and public affairs.

    They also examined government policies and suggested improvements. Native newspapers and political associations could perform a role similar to the opposition in the British Parliament by critically examining government action.

    FatwaA legal pronouncement on Islamic law, generally issued by a mufti.
    MonotheismBelief in one God.
    VernacularLanguage spoken by ordinary people in a region.
    9. NEW FORMS OF PUBLICATION

    9.1 Growth of New Literary Forms

    • Printing created demand for new kinds of writing.
    • Readers wanted to see their own lives, emotions and relationships reflected in books.
    • The novel, which developed in Europe, became popular in India.
    • Indian novels developed distinctive Indian forms and styles.
    • Other forms included lyrics, short stories and essays on social and political issues.

    9.2 Development of Visual Culture

    • By the end of the nineteenth century, a new visual culture was developing.
    • Printing presses reproduced visual images in multiple copies.
    • Painters produced images for mass circulation.
    • Wood engravers made printing blocks.
    • Cheap prints and calendars became available in bazaars.
    • Poor people could buy prints to decorate their homes and workplaces.
    • These images shaped ideas about modernity, tradition, religion, politics, society and culture.

    9.3 Raja Ravi Varma

    • Raja Ravi Varma was an important painter.
    • He produced numerous mythological paintings.
    • His works were printed at the Ravi Varma Press.
    • His paintings became available to a much wider public through reproduction.

    9.4 Cartoons and Caricatures

    • From the 1870s, cartoons and caricatures appeared in newspapers and journals.
    • Some criticised educated Indians' fascination with Western clothing and tastes.
    • Some expressed fear about social change.
    • Imperial caricatures mocked nationalists.
    • Nationalist cartoons criticised imperial rule.
    10. WOMEN AND PRINT

    10.1 Expansion of Women's Reading

    • Women's lives and feelings began to be described in greater detail.
    • Women's reading increased in middle-class homes.
    • Liberal husbands and fathers educated women at home.
    • Women's schools were established in towns and cities after the mid-nineteenth century.
    • Journals published writings supporting women's education.
    • Some journals supplied syllabuses and reading material for home-based schooling.

    10.2 Opposition to Women's Education

    • Some conservative Hindus believed that a literate girl would become a widow.
    • Some Muslims feared educated women would be corrupted by Urdu romances.
    • Some women secretly learnt to read and write despite restrictions.

    10.3 Rashsundari Debi

    • She lived in East Bengal.
    • She was a young married woman in an orthodox household.
    • She secretly learnt to read in her kitchen.
    • She wrote the autobiography Amar Jiban.
    • It was published in 1876.
    • It was the first full-length autobiography published in Bengali.

    10.4 Kailashbashini Debi

    • From the 1860s, Bengali women such as Kailashbashini Debi wrote about women's lives.
    • She described women's confinement within homes.
    • She highlighted their lack of education.
    • She wrote about hard domestic labour and unjust treatment.

    10.5 Tarabai Shinde

    • She wrote in the 1880s.
    • She criticised the miserable conditions of upper-caste Hindu women.
    • She particularly highlighted the suffering of widows.

    10.6 Pandita Ramabai

    • She wrote about the suffering of upper-caste Hindu women.
    • Her writings expressed strong criticism of the condition of widows.

    10.7 Growth of Hindi Printing

    • Urdu, Tamil, Bengali and Marathi print cultures had developed earlier.
    • Hindi printing began seriously only from the 1870s.
    • A large part of Hindi print literature soon focused on women's education.

    10.8 Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain

    • She was an educationist and literary figure.
    • In 1926, she addressed the Bengal Women's Education Conference.
    • She strongly criticised men who denied women education in the name of religion.
    • She argued that women had an equal right to education.

    10.9 Women's Journals in the Early Twentieth Century

    • Journals written for women and sometimes edited by women became popular.
    • They discussed women's education, widowhood and widow remarriage.
    • They covered the national movement.
    • Some offered household and fashion lessons.
    • They provided entertainment through short stories and serialised novels.

    10.10 Print Culture in Punjab

    • Folk literature was widely printed in Punjab from the early twentieth century.
    • Ram Chaddha published Istri Dharm Vichar.
    • The book taught women how to be obedient wives.
    • The Khalsa Tract Society published cheap booklets with similar messages.
    • Many were written as dialogues about the qualities of a good woman.

    10.11 Battala in Bengal

    • Battala was an area in central Calcutta devoted to popular book printing.
    • Cheap religious tracts and scriptures were sold there.
    • Some publications contained literature considered obscene or scandalous.
    • By the late nineteenth century, books were illustrated with woodcuts and coloured lithographs.
    • Pedlars carried Battala publications to homes.
    • This enabled women to read books during their leisure time.

    10.12 Images and Social Anxiety

    • Some woodcuts represented fear about changes in traditional family roles.
    • The image titled Ghor Kali depicted the destruction of traditional family relationships.
    • Another woodcut depicted an Indian couple in roles that challenged traditional expectations.
    • These images reflected anxiety about Western cultural influence and women's education.
    11. PRINT AND THE POOR PEOPLE

    11.1 Cheap Books and Access to Reading

    • Very cheap small books were brought to nineteenth-century Madras towns.
    • They were sold at crossroads and marketplaces.
    • Poor people travelling to markets could buy them.
    • Public libraries were established from the early twentieth century.
    • Libraries were mainly located in cities and towns, and sometimes in prosperous villages.
    • Rich local patrons sometimes established libraries to acquire prestige.

    11.2 Print and Caste Discrimination

    • From the late nineteenth century, caste discrimination was discussed in printed tracts and essays.
    • Local protest movements published journals and pamphlets.
    • These publications criticised ancient scriptures and imagined a more just future.

    11.3 Jyotiba Phule

    • He was a Maratha pioneer of low-caste protest movements.
    • He wrote Gulamgiri in 1871.
    • He criticised the injustices of the caste system.

    11.4 B. R. Ambedkar

    • He wrote powerfully against caste discrimination in Maharashtra.
    • His writings were read by people across India.

    11.5 E. V. Ramaswamy Naicker – Periyar

    • He was popularly known as Periyar.
    • He wrote against caste inequality in Madras.
    • His writings were read across India.

    11.6 Workers and Print

    • Factory workers were often overworked.
    • Many lacked the education needed to write about their experiences.
    • Some workers nevertheless used print to express their views.

    11.7 Kashibaba

    • He was a Kanpur millworker.
    • He wrote Chhote Aur Bade Ka Sawal in 1938.
    • The work explained the connection between caste and class exploitation.

    11.8 Sudarshan Chakr

    • He was another Kanpur millworker who wrote poetry.
    • His poems were written between 1935 and 1955.
    • They were collected in a book titled Sacchi Kavitayan.

    11.9 Workers' Libraries

    • By the 1930s, Bangalore cotton millworkers established libraries.
    • They followed the example of Bombay workers.
    • Social reformers sponsored these libraries.
    • The objectives included literacy and self-education.
    • Some libraries also propagated nationalism.
    • Reformers sometimes attempted to discourage excessive drinking among workers.

    11.10 Lakshminath Bezbaruah

    • He lived from 1868 to 1938.
    • He was an important figure in modern Assamese literature.
    • His notable work included Burhi Aair Sadhu (Grandma's Tales).
    • He wrote the popular song of Assam, O Mor Apunar Desh.
    12. PRINT AND CENSORSHIP IN INDIA

    12.1 Early Colonial Censorship – Before 1798

    • Before 1798, the East India Company was not very concerned with censorship.
    • Early restrictions were directed mainly against Englishmen in India who criticised Company misrule.
    • The Company feared that critics in England might use such reports against its trade monopoly in India.

    12.2 Press Regulations in the 1820s

    • The Calcutta Supreme Court passed regulations to control press freedom.
    • The Company encouraged newspapers that celebrated British rule.

    12.3 Press Reforms of 1835

    • Editors of English and vernacular newspapers submitted urgent petitions.
    • Governor-General Bentinck agreed to revise press laws.
    • Thomas Macaulay formulated new rules.
    • These rules restored earlier freedoms.

    12.4 Changes After the Revolt of 1857

    • After the Revolt of 1857, British attitudes towards press freedom changed.
    • Many Englishmen demanded strict control over the native press.
    • Vernacular newspapers became increasingly nationalist.
    • The colonial government began considering stringent controls.

    12.5 Vernacular Press Act, 1878

    Very Important: The Vernacular Press Act was passed in 1878 and was modelled on the Irish Press Laws.

    Main provisions:

    1. It gave the government extensive powers to censor reports and editorials in vernacular newspapers.
    2. The government regularly monitored vernacular newspapers in different provinces.
    3. If a report was considered seditious, the newspaper could receive a warning.
    4. If the warning was ignored, the printing press could be seized.
    5. Printing machinery could be confiscated.

    12.6 Nationalist Newspapers and Government Repression

    • Despite repressive measures, nationalist newspapers increased in number.
    • They reported colonial misrule.
    • They encouraged nationalist activities.
    • Attempts to suppress criticism provoked protests.
    • Protests were followed by further government persecution.

    12.7 Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Kesari

    • Punjab revolutionaries were deported in 1907.
    • Tilak wrote sympathetically about them in his newspaper Kesari.
    • He was imprisoned in 1908.
    • His imprisonment provoked widespread protests throughout India.

    12.8 Loyalist Newspapers

    • The colonial government sometimes found it difficult to recruit editors for loyalist newspapers.
    • Sanders, editor of The Statesman, questioned how much he would be paid for losing his freedom.
    • Friend of India refused a government subsidy because it feared that accepting money would force obedience to government orders.

    12.9 Censorship During the First World War

    • The Defence of India Rules were used to control newspapers.
    • 22 newspapers were required to furnish securities.
    • 18 newspapers closed rather than comply with the orders.

    12.10 Rowlatt and the Sedition Committee Report, 1919

    • The Sedition Committee Report under Rowlatt strengthened controls.
    • Penalties were imposed on various newspapers.

    12.11 Censorship During the Second World War

    • The Defence of India Act allowed censorship of reports relating to war.
    • Reports concerning the Quit India Movement also came under its purview.
    • In August 1942, approximately 90 newspapers were suppressed.

    12.12 Gandhi and Freedom of the Press

    • In 1922, Gandhi argued that the struggle for Swaraj was also a struggle for freedom of expression.
    • He emphasised liberty of speech.
    • He emphasised liberty of the press.
    • He emphasised freedom of association.
    • He considered these freedoms essential for expressing and developing public opinion.
    Key Point: The colonial government used censorship to suppress criticism, while nationalist newspapers used print to expose colonial misrule and spread political awareness.

    19. NCERT DISCUSSION QUESTIONS – WITH ANSWERS

    Q1. Why did some people in eighteenth-century Europe think that print culture would bring enlightenment and end despotism?

    Answer:
    • People believed books could spread knowledge, education and rational thinking.
    • Enlightenment thinkers such as Voltaire and Rousseau criticised superstition, traditional authority and despotism.
    • Printed books made their ideas accessible to a wider public.
    • Print encouraged discussion and debate about existing social and political institutions.
    • People began questioning the authority of the Church and absolute rulers.
    • Public opinion was increasingly seen as a force capable of challenging tyranny.
    Conclusion: Print was considered a means of spreading reason, enlightenment and freedom.

    Q2. Why did some people fear the effect of easily available printed books? Give one example from Europe and one from India.

    Answer:

    Some people feared that uncontrolled printing could spread rebellious, irreligious and anti-authority ideas. They worried that ordinary people might question established religious and political institutions.

    European example:

    The Roman Catholic Church feared the spread of dissenting religious ideas. Menocchio developed his own interpretation of the Bible after reading printed books. The Church punished him and began maintaining the Index of Prohibited Books in 1558.

    Indian example:

    The British colonial government feared nationalist criticism in vernacular newspapers. It passed the Vernacular Press Act in 1878, which allowed censorship and the seizure of printing presses.

    Q3. What were the effects of the spread of print culture for poor people in nineteenth-century India?

    Answer:
    • Cheap books became available in markets and at crossroads.
    • Poor people could purchase small books at affordable prices.
    • Public libraries expanded access to reading material.
    • Printed tracts and essays discussed caste discrimination.
    • Jyotiba Phule used print to criticise caste injustice through Gulamgiri.
    • Ambedkar and Periyar used writing to challenge caste inequality.
    • Workers such as Kashibaba and Sudarshan Chakr expressed their experiences through literature.
    • Workers' libraries encouraged literacy and self-education.
    Conclusion: Print helped poor people gain access to knowledge, express their experiences and participate in social reform.

    Q4. Explain how print culture assisted the growth of nationalism in India.

    Answer:
    • Newspapers spread information about colonial policies and government actions.
    • Printed material exposed colonial misrule.
    • Nationalist newspapers encouraged political awareness.
    • Print connected people living in different regions of India.
    • Newspapers created discussion about common political issues.
    • Printed literature helped people question colonial authority.
    • Nationalist cartoons and caricatures criticised imperial rule.
    • Leaders such as Bal Gangadhar Tilak used newspapers like Kesari to express nationalist views.
    • Attempts to suppress the press often provoked protests.
    Conclusion: Print helped spread political awareness, connect people and strengthen nationalist ideas.
    20. NCERT WRITE IN BRIEF – ALL QUESTIONS WITH ANSWERS

    Q1(a). Why did woodblock printing only come to Europe after 1295?

    Answer: Woodblock printing had developed in China much earlier. In 1295, Marco Polo returned to Italy after exploring China and brought knowledge of Chinese woodblock printing. Italians then began producing books using woodblocks, and the technique gradually spread to other parts of Europe.

    Q1(b). Why was Martin Luther in favour of print and why did he praise it?

    Answer: Martin Luther's religious ideas spread rapidly because his writings were printed and reproduced in large numbers. His Ninety Five Theses helped stimulate debate about the practices of the Roman Catholic Church. His translation of the New Testament also sold thousands of copies. He therefore considered printing a great gift because it helped spread religious ideas and reformist thinking.

    Q1(c). Why did the Roman Catholic Church begin keeping an Index of Prohibited Books from the mid-sixteenth century?

    Answer: The Church feared that printed books would encourage people to develop individual interpretations of religion and question established beliefs. The case of Menocchio, an Italian miller who developed his own interpretation of the Bible, increased these concerns. The Church therefore imposed controls on publishers and booksellers and began maintaining the Index of Prohibited Books in 1558.

    Q1(d). Why did Gandhi say that the fight for Swaraj was a fight for liberty of speech, liberty of the press and freedom of association?

    Answer: Gandhi believed that freedom of speech and the press was necessary for people to express opinions and criticise government policies. Freedom of association allowed people to organise and work together. The colonial government attempted to restrict these freedoms to suppress public opinion and nationalist activities. Therefore, Gandhi considered these freedoms essential to the struggle for Swaraj.

    Q2. Write short notes on the following.

    (a) The Gutenberg Press

    Answer: Johann Gutenberg developed the first-known European printing press in the 1430s and perfected his system by 1448. He adapted the olive press and used metal moulds to cast movable letters. His first printed book was the Bible. About 180 copies were produced in approximately three years. The press made book production faster and helped bring about the Print Revolution.

    (b) Erasmus's idea of the printed book

    Answer: Erasmus was a Latin scholar and Catholic reformer. In his work Adages (1508), he expressed concern about the excessive number of printed books. He believed that although some books contained useful knowledge, many were foolish, ignorant, scandalous, irreligious or seditious. He feared that the large number of worthless books would damage scholarship and reduce the value of useful publications.

    (c) The Vernacular Press Act

    Answer: The Vernacular Press Act was passed in 1878 by the British colonial government. It was modelled on the Irish Press Laws. It gave the government extensive powers to censor vernacular newspapers. Newspapers considered seditious could be warned. If they ignored the warning, their printing presses could be seized and their machinery confiscated. The Act was introduced to suppress nationalist criticism.

    • Writers such as Rashsundari Debi, Tarabai Shinde and Pandita Ramabai expressed women's experiences.
    • Publications discussed widowhood, widow remarriage and social restrictions.
    • Print helped women express their ideas and question social inequalities.

    (b) The Poor

    Answer:
    • Cheap books became available in markets.
    • Public libraries expanded access to reading.
    • Printed literature spread awareness of caste discrimination.
    • Workers used print to express their experiences.
    • Workers' libraries encouraged literacy and self-education.

    (c) Reformers

    Answer:
    • Reformers used print to spread social and religious reform ideas.
    • They criticised harmful social practices.
    • They published arguments in newspapers, books and pamphlets.
    • They reached a wider public.
    • They encouraged debate about caste, women's education and religious beliefs.
    21. NCERT DISCUSS, PROJECT AND ACTIVITY QUESTIONS – ANSWERS

    Q1. Why did some people fear that the development of print could lead to the growth of dissenting ideas?

    Answer: Print allowed ideas to circulate beyond the control of religious and political authorities. People could read different interpretations of religion and society. This could encourage criticism of traditional beliefs and institutions. Authorities feared that rebellious and irreligious ideas might spread, weakening their power.

    Q2. Why did some people in eighteenth-century Europe believe books could change the world?

    Answer: Books spread scientific knowledge, rational thinking and Enlightenment ideas. They encouraged people to question superstition, despotism and traditional authority. Writers such as Voltaire and Rousseau helped popularise ideas of reason, equality and criticism of absolute power.

    Q3. What was the importance of the Penny Magazine?

    Answer: The Penny Magazine was published in England between 1832 and 1835 by the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge. It was aimed primarily at working-class readers and helped make reading material accessible to ordinary people.

    Q4. What was the role of Battala in the development of print culture?

    Answer: Battala in central Calcutta became an important centre of popular book printing. It sold cheap religious tracts, scriptures and other popular literature. Books were often illustrated with woodcuts and coloured lithographs. Pedlars carried these books to homes, increasing access to printed material.

    Q5. What changes did printing bring to women's lives in India?

    Answer: Printing encouraged women's education and reading. Journals discussed women's rights, widowhood, widow remarriage and social restrictions. Women began writing autobiographies and books about their lives. Print provided women with a platform to express their experiences and challenge social inequalities.

    Q6. How did print help the growth of anti-caste movements?

    Answer: Printed books, essays and journals spread criticism of caste discrimination. Jyotiba Phule wrote Gulamgiri in 1871. B. R. Ambedkar and Periyar also used writing to challenge caste inequality. These publications helped spread anti-caste ideas across India.

    Q7. What changes took place in printing technology during the last hundred years?

    Answer: Based on the technological developments discussed in the chapter, printing gradually shifted from manual presses to metal, power-driven and electrically operated machinery. Offset printing, improved paper feeding, better printing plates, automatic paper reels and colour registration controls increased speed and quality. Modern developments have further introduced digital composition, computer-based design, laser printing and high-speed digital production.
    The final examples of digital printing are additional modern context; the historical developments in the earlier part of the answer follow the NCERT chapter.
    22. FINAL CHAPTER SUMMARY AND CONCEPTUAL FLOW

    The Journey of Print Culture

    East Asia: China, Japan and Korea developed early hand-printing techniques.
    Europe: Marco Polo carried knowledge of woodblock printing to Italy; Gutenberg developed the movable-type press.
    Print Revolution: Books became cheaper and a wider reading public emerged.
    Religion: Print encouraged religious debates and contributed to the Protestant Reformation.
    Enlightenment: Printed books spread reason, scientific knowledge and criticism of despotism.
    Social Change: Print expanded education and reading among children, women and workers.
    India: Printing spread through missionaries, newspapers, religious publications and Indian publishers.
    Reform: Print helped spread debates about caste, religion and women's education.
    Nationalism: Newspapers and printed literature spread awareness of colonial rule.
    Censorship: Colonial laws, especially the Vernacular Press Act of 1878, attempted to control the press.

    Most Important Conclusion

    Print transformed the modern world by expanding access to knowledge, encouraging debate, supporting social reform, challenging authority and helping the growth of nationalism.
