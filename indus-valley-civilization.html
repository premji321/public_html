01. Introduction

The Indus Valley Civilization, also known as the Harappan Civilization, was one of the earliest major urban civilizations of the world. It flourished in the northwestern Indian subcontinent during the Bronze Age.

It was contemporary with civilizations such as Mesopotamia and ancient Egypt. Its Mature Harappan phase is generally dated to approximately 2600–1900 BCE.

BPSC Important: Harappan Civilization is a broader geographical term because its settlements extended far beyond the Indus Valley.

03. Chronology and Important Phases

PhaseApproximate PeriodMain Features

Early Harappan Phase

  • Growth of agricultural settlements.
  • Development of specialized crafts.
  • Expansion of regional trade.
  • Emergence of fortified settlements.

Mature Harappan Phase

  • Large cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro.
  • Planned streets and drainage systems.
  • Standardized weights and measures.
  • Long-distance trade and widespread seal use.

Late Harappan Phase

  • Decline of several large urban centres.
  • Changes in trade and pottery.
  • Population redistribution.
  • Development of regional cultures.
Remember: The decline of major urban centres did not mean the complete disappearance of Harappan populations.

06. Town Planning

Town planning was one of the most remarkable features of the Harappan Civilization. Major cities displayed organized streets, residential blocks, drainage systems and standardized construction practices.

Grid Pattern

  • Many streets intersected at approximately right angles.
  • Residential blocks were arranged along streets.
  • Main roads and smaller lanes formed connected networks.
  • Houses frequently had courtyards.

Citadel and Lower Town

Many major settlements had an elevated or fortified area and a lower settlement area. Their exact functions remain uncertain.

Building Materials

  • Baked bricks.
  • Mud bricks.
  • Stone at certain sites.
  • Wood and other perishable materials.
Important Fact: Harappan bricks commonly followed a proportion of approximately 1:2:4 in thickness, width and length.

Houses

  • Rooms arranged around courtyards.
  • Brick walls and bathing areas.
  • Staircases in some houses.
  • Doors frequently opened onto side streets.
  • Some houses had access to drains.

Public Structures

  • Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro.
  • Large halls and platforms.
  • Storage-like structures.
  • Reservoirs at Dholavira.
Some structures traditionally called granaries have uncertain functions. Archaeological interpretations may change as new evidence emerges.

07. Drainage System and Sanitation

Several Harappan settlements had carefully constructed drainage systems connecting household wastewater arrangements with street drains.

Main Features

  • Brick-built drains.
  • Covered drains in many locations.
  • Gradients designed to move wastewater.
  • Household bathing areas.
  • Inspection openings or removable covers in some systems.
  • Connections between domestic and street drainage in several settlements.

Importance

  1. Improved the removal of wastewater.
  2. Demonstrated planned urban construction.
  3. Required coordination between houses and public streets.
  4. Reflected shared engineering practices.
Mains Point: Harappan drainage systems demonstrate the importance of public infrastructure in early urban civilization.

08. Great Bath of Mohenjo-daro

Location

Mohenjo-daro, Sindh, Pakistan.

Main Features

  • Rectangular water tank.
  • Baked-brick construction.
  • Waterproofing using bituminous material.
  • Steps leading into the tank.
  • Surrounding rooms and structures.
  • Arrangements for supplying and draining water.

Possible Purpose

It is often interpreted as a structure used for ritual bathing or other collective activities. Its exact function cannot be established with certainty.

BPSC One-Liner: The Great Bath was discovered at Mohenjo-daro.

10. Animal Husbandry

Animal husbandry supported food production, transportation, agricultural work and craft activities.

Important Animals

  • Cattle and buffaloes.
  • Sheep and goats.
  • Pigs and dogs.
  • Elephants.
  • Birds and other animals.
  • Camels in some regional contexts.

Humped Bull

The humped bull appears frequently in Harappan art and seals. Cattle were important for agricultural and economic activities.

Horse

The presence and significance of the horse remain debated. Some finds have been interpreted as horse remains, but their identification and context have been disputed.

Do not claim that the horse was definitively absent from every Harappan settlement. Its role is less clearly established than that of cattle.

11. Trade and Commerce

Internal Trade

Internal trade connected urban centres, villages, craft-production centres and resource-rich regions.

  • Agricultural products.
  • Pottery and textiles.
  • Beads and ornaments.
  • Metal objects.
  • Shell products.
  • Semi-precious stones.

External Trade

  • Mesopotamia.
  • Oman.
  • Bahrain.
  • Afghanistan.
  • Iranian regions.
  • Central Asian areas.

Mesopotamian Connections

Mesopotamian texts refer to a trading region called Meluhha, which many scholars associate with the Harappan region.

Maritime Trade

Coastal settlements, Gulf connections, shell-working industries and objects associated with long-distance exchange provide evidence of maritime connections.

The basin-like structure at Lothal is commonly interpreted as a dockyard, although its precise function remains debated.

BPSC Focus: Meluhha — Mesopotamian texts; Lothal — maritime trade connections.

12. Weights and Measures

  • Cubical stone weights were commonly used.
  • Chert was used for many weights.
  • Standardized weight systems occurred across settlements.
  • Measurements supported trade and craft production.
  • Recurring numerical relationships included binary and decimal patterns.

Importance

  • Commercial organization.
  • Shared measurement practices.
  • Coordination between trading communities.
  • Standardization of economic activities.
Standardized weights are an important characteristic of Mature Harappan trade.

13. Industries and Crafts

Bead-Making

Important centres included Lothal and Chanhudaro. Materials included carnelian, agate, jasper, steatite and lapis lazuli.

Pottery

Pottery was used for cooking, storage, transport, serving food and household activities.

Metallurgy

Harappan metalworkers worked with copper and bronze. Gold, silver, lead and other metals were also used.

Shell Industry

Shell was used to produce bangles, ornaments and decorative objects.

Textile Production

Evidence of cotton cultivation indicates that textile production formed part of Harappan economic life.

Ivory and Terracotta

Ivory objects, terracotta figurines, toys, beads, animal models and small carts were produced.

Remember: Lothal and Chanhudaro are important for questions on bead-making and specialized craft production.

14. Art and Sculpture

Bronze Dancing Girl

  • Found at Mohenjo-daro.
  • Made of bronze.
  • Depicts a young female figure.
  • Shows a distinctive standing posture and numerous bangles.
  • Associated with the lost-wax casting technique.

Priest-King Sculpture

  • Found at Mohenjo-daro.
  • Made from a soft stone, commonly identified as steatite.
  • Depicts a bearded male figure with a patterned garment.
"Priest-King" is a conventional modern label. There is no conclusive evidence that the figure was actually a priest or king.

Red Sandstone Male Torso

A famous male torso made of red sandstone was discovered at Harappa.

Terracotta Figurines

Figurines depict humans and animals. Some female figurines have been interpreted as mother-goddess figures, but their exact significance is uncertain.

Animal Representations

Seals and figurines depict bulls, elephants, rhinoceroses, tigers, buffaloes and other animals.

15. Pottery

Harappan pottery was generally made on a potter's wheel. Red pottery painted with black designs is a characteristic category.

Main Features

  • Red or reddish surfaces.
  • Black geometric designs.
  • Animal and plant motifs on some vessels.
  • Different forms for storage and household use.
  • Regional and chronological variation.

Uses

  • Storage of grain and other materials.
  • Cooking and carrying water.
  • Serving food.
  • Special or ritual activities.

16. Seals and Sealings

Seals are important sources for understanding Harappan society. Many were made of steatite and carried animal motifs and short inscriptions.

Features

  • Small and portable.
  • Often square or rectangular.
  • Frequently made of steatite.
  • Animal motifs and inscriptions.
  • Some had a raised boss on the reverse.

Common Animal Motifs

  • Unicorn.
  • Humped bull.
  • Elephant.
  • Rhinoceros.
  • Tiger.
  • Buffalo.

Possible Uses

  • Identification.
  • Trade and commercial administration.
  • Marking goods.
  • Ownership or access control.
  • Symbolic purposes.

Pashupati Seal

A famous seal from Mohenjo-daro depicts a seated horned figure surrounded by animals. Some scholars have compared the figure with later representations of Shiva, but this identification remains debated.

Prelims Facts: Steatite — common seal material; Unicorn — common motif; Pashupati — debated interpretation.

17. Script and Language

The Harappan script has not been conclusively deciphered.

Main Features

  • Inscriptions are usually short.
  • Signs occur on seals, pottery, tablets and other objects.
  • Hundreds of distinct signs or sign variants have been recorded, depending on classification.
  • Many inscriptions contain only a few signs.
  • Right-to-left writing is frequently inferred for many inscriptions, although practices may have varied.

Why Has It Not Been Deciphered?

  • No widely accepted bilingual inscription.
  • Short inscriptions.
  • Unknown underlying language.
  • Difficulty interpreting the sign system.

Language

The language or languages spoken by Harappan communities remain unknown. Dravidian-related hypotheses have been proposed, but no decipherment or language identification has achieved conclusive acceptance.

BPSC One-Liner: The Harappan script remains undeciphered.

18. Religious Beliefs

Harappan religious practices are reconstructed from archaeological evidence because written inscriptions cannot yet be read.

Mother-Goddess Interpretation

Some female terracotta figurines have traditionally been interpreted as mother-goddess representations. Their exact meaning remains uncertain.

Pashupati Interpretation

The seated horned figure on a famous seal has been interpreted by some scholars as a proto-Shiva or Pashupati figure. This interpretation is debated.

Tree and Animal Symbolism

Trees and animals appear in distinctive settings on seals and other objects. Their meanings may have been religious, symbolic, economic or administrative.

Fire Altars

Structures interpreted as fire altars have been reported at Kalibangan and some other sites. Their precise function is uncertain.

Water and Ritual Bathing

The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro may have been used for ritual bathing or other collective activities.

Burial Practices

Burials and grave goods provide evidence of mortuary customs, but cannot establish the complete religious beliefs of the civilization.

Do not state that Shiva worship in its exact later form is conclusively established by the Pashupati seal.

19. Social Life

Social Organization

Differences in house sizes and grave goods suggest some degree of social differentiation. The exact social hierarchy remains uncertain.

Clothing

Cotton textiles were important. Other plant or animal fibres may also have been used, although direct evidence is limited.

Ornaments

Gold, silver, copper, shell, carnelian, faience, terracotta and other stones were used to produce ornaments.

Food

  • Wheat and barley.
  • Pulses.
  • Fruits.
  • Milk and other animal products.
  • Fish and meat in some communities.
  • Other locally available foods.

Entertainment

Terracotta toys, game pieces and other objects suggest recreational activities.

Education and Literacy

The use of signs is clear, but the literacy rate and organization of education are not known.

20. Political System and Administration

The Harappan political system is not fully understood. No universally accepted series of Harappan kings or readable royal inscriptions has been identified.

Evidence of Organization

  • Standardized bricks.
  • Standardized weights.
  • Planned cities.
  • Shared pottery and craft traditions.
  • Public infrastructure.

Was It a Monarchy?

A centralized monarchy has not been conclusively established.

Was It a Republic?

There is insufficient evidence to classify the Harappan political system as a republic.

Possible Forms of Authority

  • Regional governing authorities.
  • Urban elites.
  • Merchant groups.
  • Religious or administrative institutions.
Mains Point: Organized urban planning is clearly established, but the exact political structure remains uncertain because the script is undeciphered.

21. Science and Technology

Metallurgy

Harappans worked with copper and bronze. Gold, silver, lead and other metals were also used.

Lost-Wax Casting

The bronze Dancing Girl is a famous example of metalworking associated with this technique.

Mathematics and Measurement

Standardized weights and building dimensions suggest practical knowledge of measurement.

Civil Engineering

  • Planned streets.
  • Brick construction.
  • Drainage systems.
  • Wells.
  • Reservoirs at Dholavira.

Water Management

Dholavira is especially important for understanding water conservation in a relatively dry environment.

Transport

Terracotta cart models provide evidence of wheeled vehicles. Boats and maritime transport are inferred from coastal settlement patterns and trade connections.

22. Burial Practices

Main Features

  • Several settlements contain cemeteries.
  • Extended burials are known.
  • Grave goods occur in some burials.
  • Burial customs varied across regions.
  • Some graves show differences in treatment and grave goods.

Important Sites

Harappa: Important cemetery evidence.

Rakhigarhi: Important burial remains used to study Harappan populations and social practices.

Importance

  • Diet and health.
  • Population relationships.
  • Mortuary customs.
  • Social differences.
  • Burial-related cultural practices.

23. Relations with Other Civilizations

Mesopotamia

Mesopotamian references to Meluhha and archaeological materials associated with long-distance exchange demonstrate important connections.

Oman

Copper and maritime exchange connected the Harappan world with Oman and neighbouring Gulf regions.

Bahrain

Bahrain, often associated with ancient Dilmun, played an important role in Gulf trade networks.

Afghanistan

Afghanistan provided access to resources such as lapis lazuli. Shortugai is important evidence of connections with Central Asian resource networks.

Iran and Central Asia

These regions were connected with wider networks of raw-material exchange and cultural interaction.

Conclusion: The Harappan Civilization participated in a wide Bronze Age network connecting South Asia with western and central Asia.

25. Decline of the Harappan Civilization

The decline of the Mature Harappan urban system began around 1900 BCE. Major urban centres changed, and several settlements were abandoned or transformed.

Major Theories

1. Climate Change

Changes in rainfall and climatic conditions may have affected agriculture and settlement patterns.

2. Changes in River Systems

Changes in river courses and water availability may have affected settlements dependent on river systems.

3. Floods

Repeated flooding may have damaged some settlements, but cannot explain the decline of every Harappan centre.

4. Decline in Long-Distance Trade

Changes in trade networks may have affected urban economies and craft production.

5. Environmental and Agricultural Stress

Local ecological changes, rainfall variation and agricultural pressures may have contributed to settlement changes.

6. Population Movement

Some communities moved toward other regions, contributing to the development of regional cultures.

7. Aryan Invasion Theory

Older interpretations attributed the decline to an Aryan invasion. However, this is not accepted as a sufficient explanation for the widespread archaeological transformation.

Modern Understanding: The decline was a complex and regionally varied process involving possible environmental, economic and social factors. Harappan populations did not simply disappear.

28. Limitations of Our Knowledge

  1. Script: The Harappan script remains undeciphered.
  2. Language: The spoken language or languages remain unknown.
  3. Political System: The precise form of government is uncertain.
  4. Religion: Many interpretations rely on archaeological inference.
  5. Rulers: No universally accepted list of Harappan kings is known.
  6. Decline: The relative importance of environmental, economic and social factors remains debated.
  7. Population: Relationships with later South Asian communities remain an active research area.
Mains Point: Archaeological evidence allows us to reconstruct many aspects of Harappan life, but the undeciphered script limits our knowledge of its rulers, institutions and beliefs.

30. Important Questions for BPSC Mains

Question 1. Discuss the major features of the Indus Valley Civilization.

Introduction: The Harappan Civilization was a major Bronze Age urban civilization that flourished in the northwestern Indian subcontinent.

Main Body:

  1. Urban planning and organized settlements.
  2. Advanced drainage systems.
  3. Baked-brick architecture.
  4. Agriculture and animal husbandry.
  5. Craft production and metallurgy.
  6. Internal and external trade.
  7. Seals and undeciphered script.
  8. Art and sculpture.

Conclusion: The civilization represents a major achievement in early urban planning, craft production and economic organization.

Question 2. Explain the town-planning system of the Harappan Civilization.

Discuss planned streets, residential blocks, courtyard houses, baked bricks, drainage systems, wells and water-management structures. Conclude by emphasizing coordinated urban infrastructure.

Question 3. Discuss the economic life of the Harappan people.

Explain agriculture, animal husbandry, craft specialization, internal trade, external trade and standardized weights and measures.

Question 4. Examine the religious beliefs of the Harappan Civilization.

Discuss female figurines, the Pashupati seal, animal imagery, tree symbolism, possible fire altars, the Great Bath and burial practices. Explain the limitations of archaeological interpretation.

Question 5. Discuss the causes of the decline of the Harappan Civilization.

Examine climate change, river-system changes, floods, environmental stress, changes in trade, population movement and regional transformations.

Question 6. Explain the importance of trade in the Harappan Civilization.

Discuss the supply of raw materials, specialized crafts, standardized weights, trade with Mesopotamia and connections with Oman and Bahrain.

Mains Answer Structure: Introduction → Evidence-based explanation → Examples of sites → Analysis → Balanced conclusion.
