01 Introduction

The Indus Valley Civilization, also known as the Harappan Civilization, was one of the earliest urban civilizations of the ancient world. It developed in the north-western part of the Indian subcontinent.

Its mature urban phase flourished approximately between 2600 BCE and 1900 BCE. The civilization was remarkable for planned cities, standardized bricks, drainage systems, craft production, seals and long-distance trade.

2600–1900 Mature Harappan Phase (BCE)
1921 Harappa Excavation by Daya Ram Sahni
1922 Mohenjo-daro Excavation by R. D. Banerji
Undeciphered Harappan Script
BPSC Focus: The Harappan Civilization is especially important for questions on archaeological sites, town planning, drainage, seals, trade and distinctive discoveries.

02 Chronology and Phases

Phase Approximate Period Major Features
Early Harappan c. 3300–2600 BCE Regional settlements, early urban development and expanding exchange.
Mature Harappan c. 2600–1900 BCE Large planned cities, standardized material culture and extensive trade.
Late Harappan c. 1900–1300 BCE Urban decline and regional changes in settlement patterns.
These dates are approximate and may vary slightly between archaeological regions and scholarly chronologies.

03 Discovery and Excavations

Person Year Importance
Daya Ram Sahni 1921 Excavated Harappa.
Rakhal Das Banerji 1922 Excavated Mohenjo-daro.
John Marshall 1924 Publicly announced the discovery of the civilization.

The excavations established the existence of a major Bronze Age civilization that had previously been unknown in its urban extent.

04 Geographical Extent

Harappan settlements extended across parts of present-day Pakistan and north-western and western India. The wider cultural zone included river valleys, semi-arid regions, coastal areas and agricultural plains.

Direction / Region Important Site Present-Day Location
Western extent Sutkagen-dor Balochistan, Pakistan
Northern extent Manda Jammu region, India
Eastern extent Alamgirpur Uttar Pradesh, India
Southern extent Daimabad Maharashtra, India
Exam Note: These are commonly cited extreme sites of the broader Harappan cultural distribution. The precise extent depends on which archaeological phase and type of settlement are being considered.

Lothal Gujarat, India Craft production, bead-making and a structure commonly interpreted as a dockyard.

08 Town Planning

Urban planning was one of the most distinctive characteristics of the Mature Harappan Civilization.

  • Grid pattern: Many streets intersected at approximately right angles.
  • Baked bricks: Standardized bricks were widely used in important urban structures.
  • Residential buildings: Houses often had courtyards and rooms arranged around them.
  • Wells: Private and public wells supplied water in several settlements.
  • Drainage: Drains were integrated into the urban environment.
  • Public structures: Some settlements contained large buildings and specialized public spaces.

Citadel and Lower Town

Some major Harappan cities had an elevated or fortified sector commonly called the citadel and a lower settlement. This arrangement was not identical at every site.

BPSC Keyword: Standardization, urban planning, drainage, baked bricks and organized settlement.

09 Drainage and Sanitation

  • Many houses had bathing areas connected to drains.
  • Street drains were constructed using bricks.
  • Some drains were covered and could be accessed for cleaning.
  • Wastewater management was an important component of urban planning.
  • Wells and water facilities were present in several settlements.

The drainage system demonstrates substantial attention to the management of water and urban infrastructure.

Avoid claiming that every Harappan city had exactly the same drainage arrangement. Archaeological evidence varies by settlement and period.

10 Agriculture and Animal Husbandry

Major Crops

  • Wheat
  • Barley
  • Peas and other pulses
  • Sesame in some regions
  • Cotton
  • Rice evidence at selected sites and contexts

Animal Husbandry

Harappan communities kept cattle, sheep, goats and other domesticated animals. Evidence for animal use differs between settlements and regions.

Important Archaeological Evidence

  • Kalibangan: Evidence of a ploughed field.
  • Farm production: Supported settlements and exchange networks.
  • Floodplains: Riverine environments supported agriculture in several regions.

11 Economy and Trade

The Harappan economy combined agriculture, animal husbandry, craft production and exchange within and between regions.

Bead-making Craft workshops and finished beads Specialized production
Metallurgy Copper and bronze objects Tools, ornaments and objects
Trade Seals, standardized weights and imported materials Exchange networks
Pottery Wheel-made pottery, often painted Domestic and economic use

12 Crafts and Technology

  • Metallurgy: Copper and bronze objects were produced.
  • Bead-making: Carnelian, agate and other stones were used.
  • Pottery: Wheel-made pottery included painted designs.
  • Shell working: Shell ornaments and objects were produced.
  • Faience: Glazed non-clay material was used for ornaments and small objects.
  • Textiles: Cotton use is supported by archaeological evidence.
  • Lost-wax casting: Associated with the famous bronze Dancing Girl.

13 Weights and Measures

  • Standardized weights were used in Harappan exchange.
  • Many weights were made from stone.
  • Weight systems show substantial regularity.
  • Standardization facilitated craft production and trade.
Remember: Standardized weights are an important indicator of shared practices across Harappan settlements.

14 Art and Sculpture

Bronze Dancing Girl

The famous bronze female figurine from Mohenjo-daro is commonly called the Dancing Girl. It demonstrates the Harappans' knowledge of metal casting.

Bearded Male Sculpture

A well-known stone sculpture from Mohenjo-daro is conventionally described as the Priest-King, although this title and the person's identity are not established.

Terracotta Figurines

Terracotta figurines of humans and animals have been recovered from various Harappan settlements.

Pottery

Painted pottery, including geometric and natural motifs, forms an important part of the archaeological record.

15 Seals and Script

Seals

  • Many seals were made from steatite.
  • Animal motifs and short inscriptions frequently appear.
  • The so-called unicorn motif is particularly common.
  • Seals may have served commercial, administrative or social functions.

Harappan Script

  • The script remains undeciphered.
  • Inscriptions are generally short.
  • Signs appear on seals and other objects.
  • The language represented by the script has not been conclusively established.
Common MCQ Trap: Do not state that the Harappan script has been definitively deciphered or that its language is conclusively known.

16 Religious Beliefs

Harappan religious practices are difficult to reconstruct because the script is undeciphered and many interpretations depend on archaeological objects.

  • Female figurines have sometimes been interpreted as representations of a mother goddess, but this is debated.
  • A seal showing a seated horned figure has been interpreted by some scholars as a possible deity.
  • Animal motifs occur frequently on seals.
  • Some structures and objects may have had ritual functions, but their precise meaning is uncertain.
Distinguish archaeological evidence from later interpretations. Avoid presenting debated religious identifications as proven facts.

17 Society, Food and Daily Life

Food

Archaeological evidence suggests the consumption of cereals, pulses and other plant foods, along with animal products. Diet varied by region and community.

Clothing and Ornaments

  • Evidence includes beads, bangles, necklaces and other ornaments.
  • Cotton was used by Harappan communities.
  • Materials included shell, stone, metal and faience.

Urban Society

Differences in house size, craft production and access to goods suggest variation in wealth and social roles. However, the exact social hierarchy is not fully known.

18 Political Organisation

The political structure of the Harappan Civilization remains uncertain. No deciphered written records identify its rulers or government.

  • Standardized bricks and weights suggest shared practices.
  • Large settlements required some form of coordination.
  • Urban planning indicates organized construction and maintenance.
  • There is no conclusive evidence identifying a single ruler for all Harappan settlements.
Mains Point: Harappan urban standardization is clear, but the exact nature of political authority remains debated.

19 Burial Practices

  • Burials have been discovered at several Harappan sites.
  • Some graves contained pottery, ornaments or other objects.
  • Burial customs varied across sites and periods.
  • Grave goods provide evidence for studying social practices and beliefs.

Burial evidence should be interpreted carefully because grave objects do not automatically reveal a person's exact social status or religious identity.

20 External Trade and Mesopotamia

Harappan communities participated in long-distance exchange networks connecting different parts of the ancient Near East and the Indian subcontinent.

  • Mesopotamian texts refer to a trading region called Meluhha, commonly associated with the Indus region.
  • Harappan seals and materials have been found in wider exchange contexts.
  • Carnelian beads, shell objects and other craft products formed part of exchange networks.
  • Boats and riverine or coastal routes may have contributed to transport.
Important Connection: Harappan Civilization ↔ Mesopotamia ↔ Long-distance trade.

21 Decline and Transformation

After approximately 1900 BCE, many major urban centres changed or declined. The process was gradual and varied across regions rather than being a single uniform event.

Possible Contributing Factors

  • Changes in monsoon patterns and climate.
  • Shifts in river courses and the availability of water.
  • Changes in agricultural productivity.
  • Disruption or reorganisation of long-distance trade.
  • Movement of populations toward different settlement regions.
Modern Interpretation: There is no universally accepted single cause for the transformation of Harappan urban society. Evidence supports a complex, regionally varied process.

22 Harappan Civilization and Early Vedic Society

Basis Harappan Civilization Early Vedic Society
Chronology Mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE Early Vedic period is generally placed in the second millennium BCE, with chronology debated.
Evidence Primarily archaeological Primarily textual, especially the Rigveda, alongside archaeological evidence.
Urbanism Large planned urban settlements Early Vedic society is generally described as more pastoral and less urban.
Economy Agriculture, crafts and extensive exchange Cattle pastoralism was important; agriculture also existed.
Script Harappan script remains undeciphered Vedic compositions were transmitted orally.
This is a broad comparison of different historical contexts. It should not be used to assume that every region or community followed an identical pattern.

25 BPSC Mains Answer Framework

Question: Discuss the main features of the Harappan Civilization.

Introduction:

The Harappan Civilization was a major Bronze Age urban civilization that flourished in the north-western subcontinent, with its Mature Harappan phase dated approximately to 2600–1900 BCE.

Main Body:

  1. Urban planning: Planned streets, brick buildings and drainage systems.
  2. Economy: Agriculture, animal husbandry, specialized crafts and trade.
  3. Material culture: Seals, pottery, ornaments and metal objects.
  4. Technology: Standardized weights, brick production and metalworking.
  5. External relations: Trade connections with Mesopotamia and other regions.
  6. Historical limitations: Script undeciphered and political structure uncertain.

Conclusion:

The Harappan Civilization demonstrates the development of sophisticated urban life, economic exchange and technological practices in the ancient subcontinent.

Answer-Writing Tip: For a 150–200 word answer, include a brief introduction, four or five organised points and a balanced conclusion. Add examples of specific archaeological sites wherever relevant.
