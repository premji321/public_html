1. Introduction

The Revolt of 1857 was a major uprising against British rule in India. It began as a revolt of Indian soldiers (sepoys) in the Bengal Army but developed into a broader rebellion involving rulers, peasants, zamindars and sections of the civilian population in several regions.

It challenged the authority of the East India Company and ultimately led to the transfer of Indian administration to the British Crown in 1858.

EXAM POINT: The revolt did not begin simultaneously throughout India. Its intensity and participation varied considerably from region to region.

2. Background of the Revolt

By the middle of the nineteenth century, the East India Company had expanded its territorial control across large parts of India. British expansion created resentment among different sections of society.

The principal sources of discontent included:

  1. Annexation of Indian kingdoms.
  2. Displacement of rulers and traditional elites.
  3. Exploitative land-revenue arrangements.
  4. Economic difficulties among peasants and artisans.
  5. Discontent among Indian soldiers.
  6. Fear of interference in religious and social customs.
  7. Racial discrimination and exclusion from higher positions.

3. Causes of the Revolt of 1857

A. Political Causes

1. Doctrine of Lapse

The Doctrine of Lapse was aggressively applied during the tenure of Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856). Under this policy, the British refused to recognise certain adopted heirs as legitimate successors to rulers of states considered dependent on British authority.

2. Annexation of Awadh, 1856

Awadh was annexed during Lord Dalhousie's tenure. The British accused Nawab Wajid Ali Shah's administration of misgovernment.

  • Displacement of the Nawab.
  • Loss of privileges for sections of the aristocracy.
  • Discontent among taluqdars.
  • Resentment among soldiers, many of whom had connections with Awadh.

Awadh subsequently became one of the major centres of the revolt.

3. Disrespect Towards Indian Rulers

The treatment of former ruling families and the weakening of traditional political authority created dissatisfaction. Nana Sahib, the adopted son of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II, was denied continuation of his father's pension. He later became a prominent leader of the revolt at Kanpur.

B. Economic Causes

1. Peasants

  • Heavy land-revenue demands created financial pressure.
  • Failure to pay revenue could result in the loss of land.
  • Moneylenders gained influence over indebted peasants.
  • Revenue collection frequently intensified rural distress.

2. Artisans and Craftspeople

  • Competition from machine-made British textiles.
  • Decline in the patronage of traditional rulers.
  • Disruption of established handicraft markets.

3. Zamindars and Taluqdars

Annexations and changes in land-revenue administration deprived some landholders of their privileges. In Awadh, the displacement of taluqdars contributed significantly to rural discontent.

4. Traditional Elites

The decline of royal courts reduced employment and patronage available to soldiers, artists, craftspeople and administrators.

EXAM POINT: Economic grievances were important, but they did not produce identical responses across all regions.

D. Religious and Social Causes

  • Christian missionary activities generated suspicion among some sections.
  • Certain British policies were interpreted as interference in established customs.
  • Changes in education and administration created anxiety about cultural identity.
  • Rumours circulated that the British intended to convert Indians to Christianity.

However, the revolt was not simply a religious uprising. Political, economic and military grievances were equally important.

E. Immediate Cause: Greased-Cartridge Controversy

The immediate trigger was the introduction of the Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle. Its cartridge was opened by biting off one end before loading. Rumours spread that the cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat.

  • Cows were sacred to many Hindus.
  • Pigs were considered unclean by Muslims.

A. Delhi

After the outbreak at Meerut, rebels reached Delhi on 11 May 1857. They declared the ageing Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah II their symbolic leader. Delhi became an important political centre of the revolt.

The British recaptured Delhi in September 1857. Bahadur Shah II was subsequently arrested, tried and exiled to Rangoon, where he died in 1862.

B. Kanpur

Nana Sahib emerged as the principal rebel leader at Kanpur. Tantia Tope played a major military role in the campaign. After the British recovered Kanpur, Tantia Tope continued resistance through mobile military operations. He was captured and executed in 1859.

C. Lucknow and Awadh

Lucknow became a major centre of resistance. Begum Hazrat Mahal, the wife of Wajid Ali Shah, led opposition to British rule. Her son, Birjis Qadr, was proclaimed ruler. The resistance drew support from sections of the former royal establishment, taluqdars, soldiers and civilians.

D. Jhansi

Rani Lakshmibai became one of the most prominent figures of the revolt. Her kingdom had been annexed in 1854 under the Doctrine of Lapse. She fought against British forces and later joined the struggle in central India. She died fighting near Gwalior in June 1858.

E. Bihar: Kunwar Singh

Kunwar Singh, the zamindar of Jagdishpur in present-day Bihar, was one of the leading figures of the revolt.

  • He was approximately 80 years old in 1857.
  • He belonged to a landed aristocratic family.
  • He led resistance against British forces in Bihar.
  • Jagdishpur became an important centre of his activities.
  • He regained control of Jagdishpur in April 1858.
  • He died on 26 April 1858.

His brother Amar Singh continued resistance after his death.

BPSC SPECIAL FOCUS: Kunwar Singh is among the most important personalities to study for Bihar-specific questions on the Revolt of 1857.

5. Why Did the Revolt Fail?

ReasonExplanation
Lack of unified leadershipThere was no single central authority capable of coordinating all rebel forces.
Limited geographical spreadThe revolt was strongest in northern and central India.
Lack of a common programmeDifferent groups participated for different reasons.
British military advantagesThe British possessed advantages in organisation, communications, logistics and reinforcements.
Support from some Indian rulersSeveral princely states supported the British or remained neutral.
Limited resourcesThe rebels faced difficulties obtaining weapons, ammunition, funds and supplies.
Lack of coordinationRebel forces did not coordinate their operations effectively across different centres.
EXAM CONCLUSION: The revolt failed because of fragmented leadership, uneven participation, limited coordination and British military advantages—not because the rebels lacked courage or popular grievances.

6. Nature of the Revolt: Historical Interpretations

InterpretationExplanation
Sepoy MutinyEmphasises the role of Indian soldiers in initiating the revolt.
Popular rebellionHighlights the participation of civilians, peasants, taluqdars and displaced elites in several regions.
First War of IndependenceEmphasises its anti-colonial character and later nationalist significance.
Regional and varied uprisingRecognises that participation, objectives and intensity differed across India.

Important Historians and Writers

  • V. D. Savarkar: Popularised the interpretation of the revolt as the First War of Indian Independence through his 1909 work.
  • S. N. Sen: His work Eighteen Fifty-Seven examined the revolt's causes and development.
  • R. C. Majumdar: Emphasised the varied nature of the uprising and challenged the idea that it was uniformly a national war of independence.
EXAM CAUTION: There is no single interpretation accepted by every historian. The revolt's character must be assessed regionally and historically.

7. Consequences of the Revolt of 1857

A. End of East India Company Rule

The Government of India Act, 1858 transferred the administration of India from the East India Company to the British Crown. The Company's governing role ended.

B. Queen Victoria's Proclamation, 1858

The proclamation included assurances concerning:

  • Respect for religious beliefs and practices.
  • Non-interference in religious matters, subject to the proclamation's terms.
  • Respect for treaties with Indian princes.
  • Equal treatment under the law.
  • Eligibility for public employment subject to qualifications and other conditions.

These assurances did not establish equality in practice or end colonial discrimination.

C. Secretary of State for India

The Act of 1858 created the office of the Secretary of State for India, a member of the British Cabinet. The Secretary of State was assisted by a Council of India. This strengthened direct control from Britain.

D. Governor-General and Viceroy

Lord Canning became the first Viceroy of India under Crown rule. The Governor-General continued to administer British India, while the title Viceroy emphasised his role as the Crown's representative.

E. Changes in the Army

  • Increasing the proportion of British soldiers in key formations.
  • Greater control over artillery.
  • Recruitment policies favouring selected communities and regions.
  • Increased emphasis on preventing unified resistance among Indian soldiers.

F. Policy Towards Princely States

The British became more cautious about annexing princely states. They increasingly relied on alliances with Indian princes to maintain imperial control. Subsequent Crown policy also addressed adoption rights.

G. Changes in British Attitudes

The revolt increased racial suspicion and hostility between many British officials and Indians. Colonial administration increasingly emphasised security and the preservation of British authority.

11. Final Revision: Remember These 10 Facts

  1. 10 May 1857: Major outbreak at Meerut.
  2. 11 May 1857: Rebels reached Delhi.
  3. Bahadur Shah II: Symbolic leader of the revolt.
  4. Mangal Pandey: Associated with Barrackpore.
  5. Rani Lakshmibai: Leader associated with Jhansi.
  6. Begum Hazrat Mahal: Leader associated with Lucknow.
  7. Kunwar Singh: Major leader in Bihar.
  8. Lord Dalhousie: Associated with the Doctrine of Lapse.
  9. Lord Canning: Governor-General during the revolt and first Viceroy under Crown rule.
  10. Government of India Act, 1858: Ended Company rule and transferred administration to the British Crown.

BPSC Strategy

For this topic, prioritise three areas:

  • Chronology: Events before, during and after May 1857.
  • Leader–Centre matching: Especially Kunwar Singh, Rani Lakshmibai, Nana Sahib and Begum Hazrat Mahal.
  • Consequences: Government of India Act, 1858; Queen Victoria's Proclamation; and changes in the army and administration.
THE MOST IMPORTANT DISTINCTION: The Revolt of 1857 ended the East India Company's governing authority, but it did not end British colonial rule in India.
