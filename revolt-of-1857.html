01

Introduction

The Revolt of 1857 began as an uprising of Indian soldiers (sepoys) in the Company's Bengal Army and developed into a wider rebellion in several regions, involving rulers, peasants, zamindars, taluqdars and sections of the civilian population.

It challenged the authority of the East India Company. In 1858, administration of India was transferred from the Company to the British Crown.
Important: The revolt did not begin simultaneously throughout India. Its intensity and participation varied considerably from region to region.
02

Background

By the middle of the nineteenth century, the Company controlled large parts of India. Annexations, revenue policies, economic disruption, military discrimination and fears of cultural interference generated resentment.

Political discontent

Rulers and their families faced annexation and loss of authority.

Rural distress

Revenue pressure and changes in land relations affected peasants, zamindars and taluqdars.

Economic change

Traditional crafts and royal patronage declined as British commercial influence expanded.

Military resentment

Pay differences, promotion restrictions and anxiety over service conditions weakened trust.

03

Causes of the Revolt

Classify the causes into political, economic, military, religious-social and immediate causes.

A. Political causes: Doctrine of Lapse

Associated especially with Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856), this policy enabled annexation of certain states when a ruler died without a recognised natural male heir, where the British refused to recognise an adopted heir for succession.

  • Satara: 1848
  • Sambalpur: 1849
  • Jhansi: 1854
  • Nagpur: 1854
Do not confuse: Awadh was annexed in 1856 on grounds of alleged misgovernment, not under the Doctrine of Lapse.

Annexation of Awadh, 1856

The British deposed Nawab Wajid Ali Shah, citing alleged misgovernment. This angered sections of the aristocracy and taluqdars and affected soldiers with family connections in Awadh.

Nana Sahib's grievance

Nana Sahib, adopted son of the last Peshwa Baji Rao II, was denied continuation of his father's pension. He later became a prominent rebel leader at Kanpur.

B. Economic causes

  • Peasants: Heavy revenue demands, debt and risk of losing land increased hardship.
  • Artisans: Machine-made British goods competed with Indian products, while royal patronage declined.
  • Zamindars and taluqdars: Annexations and revenue changes deprived some landholders of privileges.
  • Traditional elites: Declining royal courts reduced employment for soldiers, artists, craftspeople and administrators.
Economic grievances mattered, but responses differed across regions.

C. Military causes

  • Indian soldiers often received lower pay and fewer privileges than British soldiers.
  • Indians faced restrictions on promotion and access to senior command.
  • The General Service Enlistment Act, 1856 required new recruits to accept the possibility of overseas service.
  • Changes in allowances and service conditions created resentment.
  • Religious and cultural rumours increased mistrust between sepoys and British officers.

D. Religious and social causes

Missionary activity, certain social and educational changes, and rumours of forced conversion created anxiety among some Indians. Some interpreted British policies as threats to established customs.

The revolt was not simply religious: political, economic and military grievances were also central.

E. Immediate cause: greased cartridges

The Pattern 1853 Enfield rifle cartridge had to be bitten open before loading. Rumours spread that cartridges were greased with cow and pig fat—offensive to many Hindu and Muslim soldiers respectively.

Major Centres and Leaders

Memorise these leader–centre pairs. Bihar-specific questions often focus on Kunwar Singh and Jagdishpur.

Delhi — Bahadur Shah II

Symbolic Mughal leader. Delhi was recaptured in September 1857. He was exiled to Rangoon and died in 1862.

Kanpur — Nana Sahib

Prominent rebel associated with the former Peshwa family and the pension dispute.

Kanpur / Central India — Tantia Tope

Major military leader who continued mobile resistance; executed in 1859.

Lucknow — Begum Hazrat Mahal

Led resistance in Awadh. Her son Birjis Qadr was proclaimed ruler.

Jhansi — Rani Lakshmibai

One of the best-known leaders; died fighting near Gwalior in June 1858.

Bihar — Kunwar Singh

Jagdishpur leader, about 80 years old in 1857. Regained Jagdishpur in April 1858 and died on 26 April 1858; Amar Singh continued resistance.

06

Why Did the Revolt Fail?

Fragmented leadership

No single central authority coordinated all rebel forces.

Uneven spread

The revolt was strongest in northern and central India; several regions saw no comparable uprising.

No common programme

Groups pursued different aims, from restoring rulers to opposing revenue and military policies.

British advantages

Military organisation, communications, logistics and reinforcements helped the British.

Support from some rulers

Some princely states backed the British or remained neutral.

Limited resources

Rebels struggled to obtain weapons, ammunition, funds and sustained supplies.

The revolt failed because of fragmented leadership, uneven participation and British military advantages—not because the rebels lacked courage or genuine grievances.

Historical Interpretations

InterpretationWhat it emphasises
Sepoy MutinyThe role of Indian soldiers in initiating the uprising.
Popular rebellionCivilian, peasant, taluqdar and displaced-elite participation in several regions.
First War of IndependenceIts anti-colonial character and later nationalist significance.
Regional and varied uprisingDifferences in participation, objectives and intensity across India.

Important writers and historians

  • V. D. Savarkar: Popularised the “First War of Indian Independence” interpretation in his 1909 work.
  • S. N. Sen: Examined causes and development in Eighteen Fifty-Seven.
  • R. C. Majumdar: Emphasised the varied nature of the uprising and challenged the view that it was uniformly a national war of independence.
Exam caution: No single interpretation is accepted by every historian. Assess the revolt in its regional and historical context.
08

Consequences of the Revolt

A. End of Company rule

The Government of India Act, 1858 transferred administration from the East India Company to the British Crown.

B. Queen Victoria's Proclamation, 1858

It announced the framework of Crown rule and assurances concerning religious beliefs, treaties with Indian princes and eligibility for public employment. These assurances did not end colonial discrimination or establish equality in practice.

C. Secretary of State for India

The Act created the office of Secretary of State for India, a British Cabinet member assisted by a Council of India, strengthening direct control from Britain.

D. Governor-General and Viceroy

Lord Canning became the first Viceroy under Crown rule. The Governor-General continued to administer British India; “Viceroy” emphasised his role as the Crown's representative.

E. Army changes

  • Increased the proportion of British soldiers in key formations.
  • Maintained tighter control over artillery.
  • Recruitment favoured selected regions and communities.
  • Army organisation aimed to prevent another coordinated uprising.

F. Princely states

The British became more cautious about annexation and relied more on alliances with Indian princes. Crown policy also addressed recognition of adoption rights.

G. Racial attitudes

The revolt intensified racial suspicion and hostility; colonial administration placed greater emphasis on security and preserving British authority.

Core distinction: The Revolt of 1857 ended the East India Company's governing authority, but it did not end British colonial rule in India.

BPSC Prelims: Practice Quiz

Try to answer each question before opening the answer.

Q1. The major outbreak began at: A) Delhi B) Meerut C) Kanpur D) Lucknow
Answer: B) Meerut — 10 May 1857.
Q2. Who was the Mughal emperor during the revolt? A) Akbar II B) Shah Alam II C) Bahadur Shah II D) Farrukhsiyar
Answer: C) Bahadur Shah II.
Q3. Awadh was annexed in 1856 on grounds of: A) Doctrine of Lapse B) Alleged misgovernment C) Subsidiary Alliance D) Customs duties
Answer: B) Alleged misgovernment.
Q4. Who led the revolt in Bihar? A) Nana Sahib B) Kunwar Singh C) Khan Bahadur Khan D) Ahmadullah Shah
Answer: B) Kunwar Singh, associated with Jagdishpur.
Q5. Who was Governor-General when the revolt began? A) Dalhousie B) Canning C) Ripon D) Curzon
Answer: B) Lord Canning.
Q6. The Government of India Act, 1858 transferred administration to: A) Mughal emperor B) British Crown C) Congress D) Princely states
Answer: B) The British Crown.
Q7. Who led resistance at Lucknow? A) Rani Lakshmibai B) Begum Hazrat Mahal C) Annie Besant D) Sarojini Naidu
Answer: B) Begum Hazrat Mahal.
Q8. The Doctrine of Lapse is most closely associated with: A) Ripon B) Dalhousie C) Curzon D) Wavell
Answer: B) Lord Dalhousie.
Q9. The immediate trigger was the: A) Rowlatt Act B) Vernacular Press Act C) Greased-cartridge controversy D) Partition of Bengal
Answer: C) Greased-cartridge controversy.
Q10. Who became the first Viceroy under Crown rule? A) Dalhousie B) Canning C) Mountbatten D) Ripon
Answer: B) Lord Canning.
Last-minute checklist: Meerut—10 May; Delhi—11 May; Mangal Pandey—Barrackpore; Kunwar Singh—Jagdishpur; Awadh—misgovernment; Doctrine of Lapse—Dalhousie; Act of 1858—Crown rule.
